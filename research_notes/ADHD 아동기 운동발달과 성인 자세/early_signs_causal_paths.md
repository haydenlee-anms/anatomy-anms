## Q1. What infant and toddler motor signs come before later ADHD, and how specific are they?

### Takeaway
Children later diagnosed with ADHD show, on average, slightly more early motor and neurological deviations, plus higher activity levels. These signs are non-specific, overlap heavily with DCD and autism, and most children with ADHD develop motor skills in the typical range. Genetic studies point the other way: higher genetic liability for ADHD goes with *earlier* walking and *better* toddler gross motor scores. Early motor delay in a child with ADHD therefore probably reflects co-occurring DCD-type or autism-type liability, or perinatal factors, rather than ADHD liability itself.

### Cited Findings
- **Athanasiadou et al. systematic review** (Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry, online 2019, print 2020). It included 9 published cohort studies with a motor assessment at or before 12 months and later ADHD symptoms. Mild early neurological markers were linked to later DCD and motor overflow movements. The authors concluded that early motor indicators "appear to be non-specific" and are not yet useful for clinical screening. Samples were small and analyses group-level. [moderate evidence that the signs are non-specific] — [Athanasiadou et al., "Early motor signs of attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder: a systematic review"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7321902/); [record](https://www.deutsche-digitale-bibliothek.de/item/2WCWJXYSXITVVQNP3KGSUQCYCR3EK7XM?lang=en)
- **Lemcke et al. 2016, Danish National Birth Cohort.**
  - Design: mothers were interviewed at 6 and 18 months. By age 8–14, 2,034 children had a registered clinical ADHD diagnosis.
  - At 6 months, deviations in motor functioning, duration of breastfeeding and incessant crying were associated with later ADHD.
  - At 18 months, "significantly more active than average" had HR 2.0 (95% CI 1.8–2.2).
  - The university press release says many of the children "began to walk later".
  - The walking-specific estimate and the U-shape (before about 11 or after about 15 months) were **not confirmed** in the summaries I could reach. (verify)
  - [moderate for "deviations exist"; weak for specific walking cut-offs] — [Lemcke et al. 2016, AAU record](https://vbn.aau.dk/en/publications/early-development-in-children-that-are-later-diagnosed-with-disor); [Aarhus University press release](https://health.medarbejdere.au.dk/en/display/artikel/adhd-and-autism-can-already-be-seen-in-toddlers)
- **Gurevitz et al. 2014** (J Atten Disord 18(1):14–22).
  - Design: retrospective case-control from Israeli "Tipat Chalav" well-baby clinic records, birth to 18 months, in 58 children later diagnosed with ADHD and 58 controls.
  - Eight infancy parameters were linked to later ADHD, including delays in motor and language development at 9 and 18 months, difficult temperament, maternal factors and head circumference.
  - The regression model explained 58% of variance. That figure is likely overfitted given n = 58 per group.
  - Mean follow-up age was only about 8 years.
  - The "general motor delay at 3 months, 44.8% vs 19%" figure the caller gave was **not confirmed** in summaries. (verify)
  - [weak; retrospective, small] — [Gurevitz et al., Bar-Ilan record](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/early-markers-in-infants-and-toddlers-for-development-of-adhd-5/); [Athanasiadou review discussing it](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7321902/)
- A related title in the results, "Motor activity at age one year does not predict ADHD at seven years", is counter-evidence for using infant activity as a predictor. Only the title was seen. [weak] — [Strathclyde record](https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/motor-activity-at-age-one-year-does-not-predict-adhd-at-seven-yea/)
- **ALSPAC (Tobarra-Sanchez et al., per search summary).**
  - In 9,201 children, high temperamental activity and motor and speech delays in the first 30 months predicted an ADHD diagnosis about 6 years later.
  - The ADHD polygenic score (PGS) added predictive information beyond these early markers.
  - Attribution of this summary to the linked page is uncertain. (verify)
  - [moderate] — [Polygenic risk scores and early manifestations of ADHD](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10242910/)
- **Gui et al. 2025, GWAS of age at onset of walking** (Nature Human Behaviour 9(7):1470–1487).
  - N = 70,560 European-ancestry infants, 11 genome-wide significant loci, SNP heritability 24.13%. The RBL2 locus colocalizes with a brain eQTL.
  - The walking-onset PGS predicted 3–5.6% of out-of-sample variance, mostly from direct effects in sib-pair analyses.
  - Walking age had a negative genetic correlation with ADHD and BMI, and positive ones with educational attainment, intelligence and gyrification in infant and adult brains.
  - One search summary gives rg(ADHD) = −0.18 (95% CI −0.24 to −0.12), described as the largest-magnitude correlation but "relatively modest", and rg(EA) = 0.119. The ADHD association reportedly held after adjusting for educational attainment (β = −0.160). (verify: numbers are from a search summary)
  - Higher ADHD genetic liability therefore goes with **earlier** walking.
  - [strong for the direction of the genetic correlation; the magnitude is modest] — [Gui et al. 2025, Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02145-1); [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.05.07.24306845v1.full); [Springer Nature community post](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/genes-that-influence-when-babies-walk-are-discovered-and-these-genes-influence-longer-term-outcomes-too); [PROGRESS news summary](https://www.progress.org.uk/genetics-may-influence-age-at-which-children-first-walk/)
- **Riglin et al. 2022, ALSPAC** (JCPP Advances).
  - N = 7,498. Motor development was measured at 18 months; language and temperament at 24 months.
  - ADHD PGS was associated with **better** gross motor scores (β = 0.04, 95% CI 0.01–0.06), higher activity (β = 0.07) and lower withdrawal (β = −0.05).
  - No PGS (ADHD, autism, schizophrenia) was strongly associated with motor or language *delays*.
  - [moderate] — [Riglin et al. 2022, JCPP Advances (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9762693/); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/e4c9a3a4cdcb4db89f4ea9022075fa17)
- **Serdarevic et al. 2020, Generation R** (Biological Psychiatry).
  - 1,174 infants had a neuromotor exam at 9–20 weeks.
  - Higher ADHD PRS was associated with less optimal "senses" items.
  - Higher autism PRS was associated with less optimal overall neuromotor development, especially **low muscle tone**.
  - [moderate] — [Serdarevic et al., EUR repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/120644); [VU record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/polygenic-risk-scores-for-developmental-disorders-neuromotor-func/)
- **MoBa, Norway (Askeland et al., per search summary).** ADHD PGS was robustly associated with hyperactivity and inattention before age 2 and with language difficulties at 5 and 8. The motor-difficulty association at 36 months was found for the autism PGS, **not** the ADHD PGS. [moderate] — [Askeland et al., BORA/UiB record](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/handle/11250/2831887?show=full)
- **DNBC study of DCD at age 7.** Intrauterine growth restriction and delayed walking were strong risk factors for DCD (an outcome separate from ADHD). [moderate] — [Determinants of DCD in 7-year-olds, DNBC](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/determinants-of-developmental-coordination-disorder-in-7-year-old)

### Inferences
- Phenotypic and genetic data diverge. Phenotypically, later-ADHD groups show more early motor deviations. Genetically, ADHD liability is linked to earlier walking and slightly better toddler gross motor skill. The two would fit together if (a) the early motor delays seen in ADHD come from co-occurring DCD- or autism-related liability, perinatal adversity, or sample selection, and (b) ADHD liability itself mostly appears as high activity. A U-shaped walking-age curve, if confirmed, would fit this: early walking for ADHD liability, late walking for comorbid motor liability. [interpretation; speculative-to-moderate]
- For an adult who "had difficulty learning movements since childhood", these data point to a co-occurring DCD-type motor-learning trait as the more likely explanation, rather than ADHD per se. [interpretation]
- Because early signs are non-specific and absent in most children with ADHD, the person's own infancy history is uninformative either way unless records exist. [interpretation]

### Gaps
- Exact walking-age cut-offs and HRs in Lemcke 2016, and the Gurevitz 3-month percentages: full texts were unreachable.
- The 2024 NDD meta-analysis of walking delay (g = 0.71) was not re-verified. Search budget ran out.
- The final published rg values in Gui 2025 still need checking against the paper (DOI 10.1038/s41562-025-02145-1).
- A MoBa paper titled "Developmental milestones in early childhood and genetic liability to neurodevelopmental disorders" ([PMC10106302](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10106302/)) turned up, but its results could not be retrieved.

## Q2. Genetics: twin heritability and genetic correlations among ADHD, DCD, motor coordination, fitness, BMI and hypermobility, and polygenic score studies

### Takeaway
Twin studies give strong evidence that ADHD and DCD/motor coordination problems share additive genetic influences. The overlap is strongest for the inattentive dimension with fine motor problems. About half of children with one condition meet criteria for the other. ADHD is also positively genetically correlated with BMI and obesity. No ADHD genetic correlation with grip strength or accelerometer-measured physical activity could be found. In general-population cohorts, ADHD PGS does not predict early motor delay.

### Cited Findings
- **Martin, Piek & Hay 2006** (Human Movement Science 25(1):110–124; Australian Twin ADHD Project).
  - Structural equation modelling found "a strong shared additive genetic component" between most ADHD subtypes and DCD.
  - On the DSM-IV-based scale, the strongest link was DCD-fine motor with ADHD-Inattentive. On the SWAN scale, general coordination was also very strongly linked.
  - The introduction cites about 7% prevalence for each condition and comorbidity "close to 50%".
  - The 1,285-pair figure and the numeric genetic correlations were not confirmed (full text unavailable).
  - A 2021 GWAS paper cites this study as giving DCD heritability of about 0.8.
  - [strong for shared genetics] — [Martin, Piek & Hay 2006, Curtin record](https://espace.curtin.edu.au/handle/20.500.11937/7425); [QxMD abstract](https://read.qxmd.com/read/16442650/dcd-and-adhd-a-genetic-study-of-their-shared-aetiology); [GWAS of motor coordination citing heritabilities](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.669902/full)
- **Lichtenstein et al. 2010** (Am J Psychiatry; Swedish CATSS).
  - All Swedish twin pairs aged 9 and 12, born 1992–2000 (N = 10,895 pairs), 80% response rate.
  - Heritability of liability was about 79% for ADHD and about 70% for DCD. MZ concordance exceeded DZ concordance for both.
  - The numeric ADHD–DCD genetic correlation was not in the abstract.
  - [strong] — [Lichtenstein et al. 2010, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20686188/)
- **Moruzzi et al. 2010, Italian Twin Registry.** DCD heritability about 0.44, as cited in a 2021 review. A companion 2011 study found clumsiness and autistic traits genetically correlated at 0.63. [moderate] — [citation in Frontiers 2021 motor-coordination GWAS](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2021.669902/full); [Moruzzi 2011, clumsiness and autistic traits](https://mijn.bsl.nl/the-nature-of-covariation-between-autistic-traits-and-clumsiness/551850)
- **Counter-evidence (VU twin study).** The genetic correlation between ADHD symptoms and computerized fine motor accuracy was low (r = −0.14). The authors concluded that fine motor control "is not a proper endophenotype for ADHD". So the shared genetics depends on the motor construct measured. [moderate] — [VU record: ADHD symptoms and fine motor control, a genetic study](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/the-relation-between-adhd-symptoms-and-fine-motor-control-a-genet/)
- **MZ co-twin control design** (J Pediatr Neurol 2008;6(3):209–219; Curtin group). Of 922 Australian MZ pairs, 866 were eligible. DCD-affected co-twins had more **oxygen perfusion complications** than unaffected co-twins. The authors concluded that pre- and perinatal factors matter more for DCD than for ADHD, implying partly different aetiological pathways. [moderate; this design controls genes and shared environment] — [Thieme abstract](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-0035-1557464); [Curtin record](https://espace.curtin.edu.au/handle/20.500.11937/20742)
- **ADHD and BMI/obesity genetic correlations.** Literature summarized in a 2020 cross-disorder paper puts rg(ADHD, BMI) at 0.21–0.26 and rg(ADHD, obesity classes) at 0.285–0.338. The same paper found a dopaminergic gene set associated with both ADHD and BMI. [strong for a positive genetic correlation] — [Cross-disorder genetic analyses: ADHD and obesity measures (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7234984/); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/573808.full.pdf)
- **ADHD and walking age:** rg is negative (see Q1). — [Gui et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-025-02145-1)
- **Grip strength.** No published LDSC genetic correlation between ADHD and hand-grip strength was found. Grip-strength GWAS papers that turned up reported correlations with cognition, not ADHD. — [Liu et al. 2025, Frontiers in Genetics](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/genetics/articles/10.3389/fgene.2025.1538308/pdf); [ALSPAC grip-strength GWAS record](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/gwas-on-hand-grip-strength-new-insights-into-the-genetics-of-musc/)
- **PGS studies in cohorts** (see Q1 for details).
  - ALSPAC (Riglin 2022): ADHD PGS linked to *better* gross motor at 18 months.
  - Generation R (Serdarevic 2020): ADHD PRS linked to "senses"; autism PRS to low tone.
  - MoBa (Askeland): motor difficulty at 36 months linked to the autism PGS, not the ADHD PGS.
  - No ADHD-PGS study on school-age motor skill or fitness (e.g., in ABCD) was found before search ran out.
  - [moderate] — [Riglin 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9762693/); [Serdarevic 2020](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/120644); [Askeland](https://bora.uib.no/bora-xmlui/handle/11250/2831887?show=full)
- **Joint hypermobility. The evidence is inconsistent.**
  - Swedish population study of 223 eleven-year-olds (Beighton cut-off ≥6): no evidence that hypermobile children had more pain or neurodevelopmental disorders.
  - A review in a Cambridge journal states that ADHD has the most robust psychiatric link to hypermobility in child cohorts, but calls it an "emerging comorbidity" with limited evidence.
  - An older small clinical study found generalized hypermobility in 32% of 54 ADHD patients vs 14% of comparisons. The exact source page of this figure was not pinned down.
  - A 2025 case-control study in children found GJH associated with anxiety disorders but not ADHD.
  - [weak overall] — [University of Gothenburg news on the Swedish study](https://www.gu.se/en/news/study-finds-no-link-between-joint-hypermobility-pain-and-neurodevelopmental-disorders-in-children-0); [Cambridge core review](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/product/F6D550242D1C362CDF89255D47E63136/core-reader); [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.803334/pdf)

### Inferences
- In someone with both ADHD and lifelong clumsiness, the most parsimonious explanation is that the same genetic liability produced both. ADHD is not shown to cause poor coordination. Shared genetics means that treating ADHD would not necessarily have prevented the motor problem. [interpretation, grounded in strong twin evidence]
- An inattentive-dominant presentation should go with more fine motor and coordination difficulty than a hyperactive-dominant one (Martin 2006; Piek studies in Q7). [interpretation; moderate]
- Hypermobility is a weak link. It is only worth considering if the person shows clinical signs (Beighton score). [speculative]

### Gaps
- Numeric ADHD–DCD genetic correlations (Martin 2006; Lichtenstein 2010): full texts unavailable.
- No twin or LDSC estimates were found for ADHD with grip strength, muscle mass, cardiorespiratory fitness or accelerometer activity. Search ran out before a dedicated query on physical-activity GWAS correlations.
- No ADHD-PGS study on school-age motor skills or fitness in ABCD, MoBa or ALSPAC was found.

## Q3. Do shared prenatal and perinatal risk factors (preterm birth, low birth weight, maternal smoking) confound the ADHD–motor link?

### Takeaway
Preterm birth and low birth weight raise the risk of **both** ADHD (OR about 2–3) and DCD (OR about 2), so they are a plausible common cause of the co-occurrence. For ADHD, the preterm association survives sibling comparison, which supports a causal contribution. Maternal smoking during pregnancy does **not** survive sibling comparison, so it is mainly a familial confounder. Perinatal oxygen complications appear to matter more for DCD than for ADHD.

### Cited Findings
- **Preterm birth and ADHD, 2024 meta-analysis** (World Journal of Psychiatry; 12 studies): OR 2.76 (95% CI 2.52–3.04), I² = 1.9%. An earlier Brazilian meta-analysis gave OR about 2.25 for very preterm or very low birth weight and about 4.05 for extremely preterm or extremely low birth weight. [strong for association] — [WJP 2024 meta-analysis](https://www.wjgnet.com/2220-3206/full/v14/i12/1956.htm); [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.00154/pdf)
- **Sibling comparisons, preterm birth and ADHD.**
  - Norwegian MoBa (JAMA Pediatrics; 113,227 children including 33,081 siblings): children born early preterm scored 0.32 SD (95% CI 0.02–0.62) higher on ADHD symptoms at 5 years than siblings born at 40 weeks.
  - A 1.2-million-child registry study found HRs rising with lower gestational age, with similar sibling-controlled results.
  - D'Onofrio et al. 2013 (Sweden) is cited as showing a dose–response independent of familial confounds. That was confirmed only second-hand. (verify)
  - [moderate-to-strong; quasi-causal design] — [HCPLive on the JAMA Pediatrics sibling study](https://hcplive.com/view/early-preterm-birth-associated-with-adhd-symptoms-especially-for-girls); [James et al. 2019, UCL, citing D'Onofrio 2013](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10075692/8/James_s%20association%20of%20preterm%20birth%20with%20cognitive-neurophysiological%20impairments%20and%20ADHD%20symptoms%20consistent%20with%20a%20causal%20inference%20or%20due%20to%20familial%20confounds_AOP.pdf); [BBRF on D'Onofrio 2013](https://bbrfoundation.org/node/10286)
- **Preterm birth and DCD.**
  - Scoping review with meta-analysis: OR 2.02 (95% CI 1.43–2.85). Preterm birth was one of only two early risk factors consistently associated with DCD.
  - 2022 systematic review of 32 studies: pooled DCD prevalence 21% in preterm children, RR about 2.2 vs term, rising as gestational age falls. Heterogeneity was high.
  - [moderate] — [Risk factors in early life for DCD: scoping review](https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/380802/risk-factors-in-early-life-for-developmental-coordination-disorder-a-scoping-review); [DCD in preterm children: systematic review](https://essopenarchive.org/doi/full/10.22541/au.169175894.49396166/v1)
- **Perinatal oxygen complications** were more frequent in DCD-affected MZ co-twins (see Q2). The authors read this as perinatal factors mattering more for DCD than for ADHD. [moderate] — [J Pediatr Neurol 2008](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-0035-1557464)
- **Intrauterine growth restriction** was a strong risk factor for DCD at age 7 in the DNBC. [moderate] — [DNBC DCD determinants](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/determinants-of-developmental-coordination-disorder-in-7-year-old)
- **Maternal smoking and ADHD.**
  - Skoglund et al. (JCPP 2014; Swedish registers, n = 813,030): estimates fell toward the null with cousin and sibling comparison (sibling HR 0.88 for moderate smoking, 0.84 for high). Conclusion: unmeasured familial confounding.
  - A Norwegian cohort (>100,000) using negative-control exposures and siblings found no within-sibling association.
  - A US sibling study (173 families) was mostly confounded, with a possible causal signal only for parent-rated hyperactive/impulsive symptoms.
  - [strong evidence against a large causal effect] — [Skoglund et al., PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25359172/); [KI open archive](https://openarchive.ki.se/articles/journal_contribution/Familial_confounding_of_the_association_between_maternal_smoking_during_pregnancy_and_ADHD_in_offspring_/27242187); [Within-family effects of smoking during pregnancy on ADHD](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/within-family-effects-of-smoking-during-pregnancy-on-adhd-the-imp/)
- **Prenatal factors and the ADHD–obesity link.** A genetically informed study reported both a shared genetic component and prenatal explanations for the ADHD-symptom–BMI association. [moderate] — [Leppert-type study: genetic and prenatal explanations (PMC8418601)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8418601/)

### Inferences
- If the person was born preterm or with low birth weight, that is one plausible common cause of both their ADHD and their motor problems. It would also carry its own musculoskeletal implications. Birth history is therefore worth asking about. [interpretation]
- Maternal smoking should not be presented as a cause of the person's ADHD or motor problems. The quasi-experimental evidence attributes the association mostly to family background. [interpretation; strong basis]

### Gaps
- No sibling-comparison study of preterm birth and **DCD or motor skill** specifically was found.
- No study was found that tests whether perinatal factors explain the ADHD–DCD *co-occurrence*, as opposed to each condition separately.

## Q4. Behavioural pathway: does poor motor competence lead to less physical activity and sport, lower fitness, and altered musculoskeletal development?

### Takeaway
Longitudinal evidence of moderate strength links childhood motor competence, especially object control, to later fitness and body composition. The relationship is probably bidirectional and non-linear (a "proficiency barrier"). Children with DCD gain relative weight and waist over time. In the general population, childhood activity predicts adolescent bone strength. Evidence that ADHD itself lowers sport participation is weak and mixed. A pooled meta-analysis reportedly finds comparable muscular strength in children with and without ADHD. I found no study connecting the chain all the way to adult *posture*.

### Cited Findings
- **Stodden et al. 2008 developmental model.** Motor competence drives physical activity through perceived competence and fitness in a reciprocal spiral across childhood. Seefeldt's (1980) "proficiency barrier" proposes a skill threshold below which children drop out of skill-based activity. [conceptual] — [JYU dissertation summarising the model](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstreams/951a6295-1ba1-4d4e-bcdc-05313ab560ed/download); [SHAPE America research summary](https://convention.shapeamerica.org/Common/Uploaded%20files/uploads/2021/prodev/research/Unlock-Research_09-2021.pdf)
- **Systematic review of 25 studies** (1996–2013, about 14,239 participants). Evidence for a direct, linear, developmentally strengthening motor-competence → activity link was mixed. The most active children were the most competent at all ages, which the authors read as consistent with a proficiency barrier. [moderate] — [AAHPERD abstract: Relationship between motor competence and physical activity, systematic review](https://aahperd.confex.com/aahperd/2014/webprogram/Paper19938.html)
- **Barnett et al. 2024** (IJBNPA; non-linear models). The object-control score–activity association was weak at low-to-mid scores and stronger at high scores, consistent with a threshold. [moderate] — [IJBNPA 2024](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com:443/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12966-023-01546-7.pdf)
- **Critique (Frontiers in Psychology 2025).** The relationship is likely bidirectional and partly mediated by perceived competence and fitness, so causality from motor competence to activity is not established. [moderate counterpoint] — [Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1480631/pdf)
- **Barnett et al. 2008** (Med Sci Sports Exerc 40(12)). Childhood object-control proficiency (assessed in 2000) predicted adolescent cardiorespiratory fitness in 2006/07 (P = 0.012), with the model explaining 26% of fitness variance. Locomotor skills did not. Attrition was large: 244 of 481 eligible completed the fitness test. [moderate] — [Deakin record](https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Does_childhood_motor_skill_proficiency_predict_adolescent_fitness_/21056533); [IJBNPA 2008 companion paper (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2569960)
- **Cairney et al. 2010** (CMAJ 182(11):1167–1172; about 2,000 Ontario 4th graders followed about 2 years; about 111 with possible DCD).
  - Children with possible DCD had higher BMI and waist circumference at baseline, and the gap persisted or widened slightly.
  - The McMaster release says they were 3× more likely to be overweight. One blog says 4× more likely to become obese (unconfirmed).
  - Activity and diet were not measured.
  - [moderate] — [McMaster news release](https://dailynews.mcmaster.ca/?p=8454); [Sharma blog commentary](https://www.drsharma.ca/clumsy-kids-more-prone-to-obesity); [HealthDay](https://consumer.healthday.com/kids-health-information-23/child-development-news-124/kids-with-coordination-disorder-more-likely-to-be-overweight-640440.html)
- **CATCH cohort (Cairney group).** It tracks body fat, aerobic and anaerobic fitness, and *foot structure* in 4–5-year-olds with probable DCD vs controls over 4 years. Baseline results examined fitness, accelerometry and adiposity. Longitudinal results were not retrieved. — [Frontiers in Pediatrics 2022, DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/e65db09a10d14db9bbc14b1da8e4d198); [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/911157)
- **Iowa Bone Development Study** (general population; Janz et al.). [moderate-to-strong for the general claim that loading during growth builds bone; not ADHD-specific]
  - Ages 4–12 (n = 468): everyday activity tracked with femoral-neck strength. Lean mass partly mediated this in girls.
  - Activity at age 5 was tested against bone mineral content at 8 and 11.
  - Ages 11–19 (N = 439): bone remained responsive to vigorous activity.
  - Activity trajectories from 5 to 17 (n = 530) were related to bone strength at 17.
  - Sources: [IBDS 2007, Bone](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Everyday-activity-predicts-bone-geometry-in/9983917771902771); [Early activity, sustained bone benefit](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Early-physical-activity-provides-sustained-bone/9983917778502771); [8-year analysis 2020](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/An-8-Year-Longitudinal-Analysis-of-Physical/9984367740502771); [BJSM trajectories](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/48/13/1032)
- **ADHD and sport participation (weak and mixed).**
  - A 2011 master's thesis (n = 330) found ADHD linked to higher odds of never playing sports (OR 5.1, wide CI). [weak]
  - An unpublished Canadian study found similar participation rates but more barriers, such as learning rules and making friends with teammates. [weak]
  - Pagani et al.: consistent organized sport in childhood predicted lower later ADHD symptoms in girls (reverse direction).
  - Sources: [USC thesis](https://scholarcommons.sc.edu/etd/1159); [NWPolytech news on Witow](https://nwpolytech.ca/news/display.html?ID=1346); [UPI on Pagani](https://www.upi.com/Health_News/2020/10/07/Early-school-sports-reduce-ADHD-symptoms-for-girls-in-later-years/1281602025092/)

The results section could not be retrieved, so the caller's "ADHD symptoms linked to higher MVPA" is **not confirmed**. (verify) — [Baltic J Sport Health Sci](https://journals.lsu.lt/baltic-journal-of-sport-health/article/view/1958)
- **Fitness in children with ADHD.**

### Inferences
- This person's history (poor sports ability and difficulty learning movements from childhood) fits the proficiency-barrier mechanism. Low motor competence leads to avoiding skill-based play and sport, which means less loading and conditioning. That plausibly leads to lower fitness, less lean mass and, in general-population data, lower bone strength. Each link is supported only by observational longitudinal data (moderate). No study tests the whole chain in ADHD, or with posture as the outcome. [interpretation; moderate for fitness and body composition, speculative for posture]
- The motor-competence deficit that drives this pathway is better documented for DCD than for ADHD alone. Children with ADHD as a group appear to have similar muscular strength (meta-analysis, unverified). The behavioural pathway is therefore most relevant if the person had DCD-level coordination problems, which their history suggests. [interpretation]
- Reverse causation is real. More activity and fitness predict *fewer* ADHD symptoms (co-twin and cohort data), so low activity may have worsened symptoms as well as resulting from them. [interpretation]

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study was found linking childhood motor competence or activity to **adult posture** (thoracic kyphosis, forward head, pelvic tilt). Search budget ran out before a dedicated query.
- No study was found on muscle mass or strength trajectories in ADHD or DCD from childhood to adulthood.
- Organized-sport participation in ADHD from a large peer-reviewed cohort was not found.

## Q5. Medication pathway: stimulant effects on growth, bone, maturation, appetite and body composition, motor control, and strength

### Takeaway
Stimulants produce a small, reasonably consistent suppression of height and weight velocity in the first 1–2.5 years (SMD about 0.27). In the MTA naturalistic follow-up, consistent use was associated with adult height about 2.4–2.6 cm lower. Bone age and pubertal timing are mostly unaffected in recent cohorts. Lower BMD in stimulant users comes from cross-sectional data only, and long-term MPH users actually have *lower* fracture risk. Acute motor effects are mixed: some children's motor scores improve on methylphenidate, many stay impaired. Evidence on strength or power is anecdotal-level.

### Cited Findings
- **Swanson et al. 2017** (J Child Psychol Psychiatry; MTA follow-up to about age 25).
  - The consistent + inconsistent use groups were 2.55 ± 0.73 cm shorter than the negligible-use group (p < .0005, d = .42).
  - Consistent users were 2.36 ± 1.13 cm shorter than inconsistent users (p < .04, d = .38).
  - Extended use was associated with height suppression but not with reduced symptom severity.
  - The authors called these analyses "hypothesis-generating" (naturalistic, non-randomized subgroups).
  - [moderate] — [Swanson et al. 2017, FPG/UNC record](https://fpg.unc.edu/publications/young-adult-outcomes-follow-multimodal-treatment-study-attention-deficithyperactivity); [Healio coverage](https://www.healio.com/psychiatry/add-adhd/news/online/%7B3e14ae45-8d00-4c04-b5f5-805b4ccb192e%7D/extended-stimulant-use-does-not-reduce-adhd-stunts-growth); [HCPLive on conflicting studies](https://www.hcplive.com/view/studies-continue-to-conflict-on-whether-adhd-meds-stunt-growth)
- **ADDUCE meta-analysis** (Carucci et al. 2021, Neurosci Biobehav Rev; 18 studies; ADHD n = 4,868; use >6 months).
  - Height deficit SMD 0.27 (95% CI 0.16–0.38).
  - The weight impact was most prominent in the first 12 months; the height impact within 24–30 months.
  - No moderation by dose, formulation, age or drug-naïve status.
  - Described as "small effect sizes with possible minimal clinical impact".
  - [strong for a small effect] — [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/444975); [UCC record](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/long-term-methylphenidate-exposure-and-growth-in-children-and-ado/)
- **Bone age and puberty.** [moderate that maturation is not substantially delayed]
  - ADDUCE 2-year cohort (Carucci et al. ~2024): no Tanner-stage differences between medicated and unmedicated ADHD. The bone-age subsample showed *slight acceleration* at 24 months, and predicted adult height stayed stable.
  - Poulton et al. 2016: growth slowed, but there was no significant maturational delay vs healthy siblings over 3 years.
  - A 2013 MJA InSight report on the same group described delayed tempo of puberty (peak height velocity timing) but not onset.
  - Sources: [ADDUCE pubertal maturation and bone age (UCL)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10187479/2/Man_ADDUCE%20pubertal%20maturation%20and%20bone%20age%2015%20oct%2023%20revised.pdf); [UCC record](https://research.ucc.ie/en/publications/the-impact-of-methylphenidate-on-pubertal-maturation-and-bone-age/); [MJA InSight 2013](https://insightplus.mja.com.au/2013/1/meds-slow-growth-adhd)
- **Bone mineral density** (Feuer et al., ENDO 2016 abstract; NHANES 2005–2010; 6,489 participants aged 8–20, of whom 159 used stimulants).
  - Users had about 5.1% lower lumbar BMC, about 3.9% lower spine BMD and about 3.7% lower hip BMD. Use longer than 6 months was worse.
  - The data are cross-sectional. As far as I found, no peer-reviewed full paper exists; the figures come from press coverage.
  - Confounding (by lower activity or lower BMI) is not addressed.
  - [weak] — [Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/285746); [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/bone-density-lower-with-use-of-adhd-stimulant-medicine)
- **Fracture risk** (Chen et al. 2017, PLoS ONE; Taiwan NHIRD; 6,201 ADHD patients diagnosed under 18). MPH for more than 180 days was associated with **lower** fracture risk (aHR 0.77, 95% CI 0.63–0.94). Shorter use showed no difference. This is probably mediated by fewer injuries, not bone strength. [moderate, observational] — [Chen et al. 2017 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5351966/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/1041729e178e4ce2a3a0f9457239c497)
- **Appetite, BMI trajectory and later obesity** (Schwartz et al. 2014, Pediatrics; EHR of 163,820 children aged 3–18).
  - Stimulant users had slower BMI growth in early childhood, then a rebound to **above** controls by late adolescence. Earlier and longer use gave stronger effects.
  - Untreated ADHD showed faster BMI growth after age 10.
  - [moderate, observational] — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/03/140318113821.htm); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20140320/idc0314schwartz_10_3928_1081_597x_20140301_19_1337741)
- **Medication status and obesity** (Cortese 2016 meta-analysis). In medicated individuals the ADHD–obesity association was non-significant; in unmedicated individuals it was significant. Obesity rates were about 40% lower in treated vs untreated (12 studies; correlational). A 2022 meta-analysis (8 studies) found OR 0.65 for obesity in treated vs untreated ADHD. [moderate, observational] — [Cortese 2016, AJP](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15020266); [Saudi Med J 2022 meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9749676/)
- **Motor control.**
  - Kaiser et al. 2015 systematic review (Res Dev Disabil; ages 5–18): more than half of children with ADHD scored low on motor tests, and many remained below norms on medication. The caller's "28–67% reach normal range" was **not confirmed** here. [moderate] — [Kaiser et al. 2015, Fribourg repository](https://folia.unifr.ch/global/documents/100146)
  - Bart et al. 2010 (secondary summary): MABC scores improved on MPH vs placebo, including balance, throwing and pegboard. [weak; verify] — [summary](https://behavioristbookclub.com/aba-research/assessment-research/a-preliminary-study-on-the-effect-of-methylphenidate-on-motor-performa/)
  - Iranian double-blind crossover, 17 children with ADHD + DCD: motor function improved in 26.6%. [weak] — [PubMed 28761201](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28761201/)
  - 2025 randomized crossover in 35 adults: no effect of a single MPH dose on balance or ball agility. [weak-moderate, adults] — [European Psychiatry 2025 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12420413/)
  - A balance review (thesis) found balance dysfunction in most studies, with medication seeming to improve postural control. [weak] — [PUC-SP thesis record](https://bdtd.ibict.br/vufind/Record/PUC_SP-1_0abb025a21d5e9e9436daa466d682157)
- **Strength and power** (Meckel, Nemet & Eliakim 2011; Acta Kinesiologiae Universitatis Tartuensis 17; 16 treated vs 16 untreated, about 17 years old). Untreated participants did better on standing long jump (p < 0.02). Sit-ups, pull-ups, shuttle run and 2,000 m did not differ. This is cross-sectional with a tiny sample. [weak] — [Meckel et al. 2011](https://ojs.utlib.ee/index.php/AKUT/article/view/akut.2011.17.09)

### Inferences
- In the scale of body shape, medication's best-documented effect is a few centimetres of possible adult height deficit in consistent long-term users. That deficit is small and not clearly causal. It would not plausibly produce "poor posture". [interpretation]
- Medication may affect body composition in two directions: appetite suppression lowers BMI during treatment, with a possible rebound later. That makes it a modifier of the BMI pathway (Q6), not a direct musculoskeletal cause. [interpretation; moderate]
- Medication probably does **not** explain lifelong motor-learning difficulty. The difficulty predates treatment, and medication improves rather than impairs motor test scores in some children. [interpretation]
- Whether this person was medicated, from what age and for how long, decides whether this pathway applies at all. [interpretation]

### Gaps
- Peer-reviewed BMD studies (beyond the 2016 NHANES abstract) and any longitudinal DXA studies of lean or fat mass under stimulants: search budget ran out.
- No study of stimulants and muscle mass or strength development in children was found.
- Exact Kaiser 2015 proportions were not verified.

## Q6. Body-composition pathway: ADHD and obesity, and implications for strength and posture

### Takeaway
ADHD is modestly associated with obesity (children OR about 1.2, adults OR about 1.55). The two are positively genetically correlated (rg about 0.2–0.3). Mendelian randomization (MR) more consistently supports **BMI → ADHD** than ADHD → BMI. Twin-difference analyses suggest much of the overlap is confounding. The ADHD → BMI pathway is therefore modest and only partly causal. No study was found linking ADHD-related adiposity to posture.

### Cited Findings
- **Cortese et al. 2016** (Am J Psychiatry 173(1):34–43; 42 studies; 728,136 individuals, 48,161 with ADHD). Pooled OR for obesity was 1.20 (95% CI 1.05–1.37) in children and 1.55 (1.32–1.81) in adults. The association was robust to study-quality and setting moderators. [strong for association] — [PsychiatryOnline](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15020266); [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/380058); [Cortese & Tessari 2017 update](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11920-017-0754-1)
- **Martins-Silva et al. 2019, bidirectional two-sample MR** (Int J Obes; ADHD GWAS N = 55,374). Higher BMI increased ADHD risk (IVW β = 0.324, 95% CI 0.198–0.449). There was no consistent evidence of ADHD liability → BMI. [moderate] — [Int J Obes 2019](https://www.doi.org/10.1038/S41366-019-0346-8)
- **Liu et al. (TEDS; medRxiv 2020, later Int J Epidemiol).** Twin-difference, PGS and MR were combined. MR suggested bidirectional effects but "substantial confounding". Twin-difference analyses gave little support for cross-lagged ADHD symptom–BMI effects over time. [moderate; triangulation] — [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2020.04.16.20067918.full.pdf); [UCL accepted manuscript](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10116487/1/Accepted_IJE_manuscript_ADHD_BMI0924.pdf)
- **2-sample MR of ADHD on physical-health outcomes.** ADHD liability → childhood obesity OR 1.29 (95% CI 1.02–1.63), and coronary artery disease OR 1.11. There was also evidence for a bidirectional ADHD–childhood obesity relationship. [moderate] — [The Effect of ADHD on Physical Health Outcomes, 2-sample MR (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8168225/)
- **A 2022 review of about 50 ADHD MR studies** concluded that physical-health MR suggests bidirectional causal effects with higher BMI, stronger for childhood obesity. [moderate] — [Cardiff ORCA review record](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/152724/)
- **DCD is linked to rising BMI and waist** (Cairney 2010, see Q4). [moderate] — [McMaster release](https://dailynews.mcmaster.ca/?p=8454)
- **Untreated ADHD showed faster BMI growth after age 10; stimulants modify the trajectory** (Schwartz 2014, see Q5). — [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/03/140318113821.htm)

### Inferences
- Higher fat mass lowers *relative* strength (strength per kg) and fitness test scores even when absolute strength is normal. This is general exercise-physiology reasoning; no ADHD-specific study was found. It could add to "very poor sports ability" in a heavier child. [speculative-to-moderate, based on general knowledge, not on a source retrieved here]
- The ADHD → BMI causal effect is small and contested. Unless this person was overweight as a child, the body-composition pathway is unlikely to be a *major* driver of their current posture. [interpretation]

### Gaps
- No study was found linking ADHD- or DCD-related childhood adiposity to adult postural alignment.
- No absolute vs relative strength analyses in ADHD cohorts were found.

## Q7. Other mediators (sleep, nutrition), measurement artifacts, and posture-specific evidence

### Takeaway
Motor deficits in ADHD correlate mainly with **inattention**, not hyperactivity. Some studies suggest the deficits shrink when comorbid autism traits are excluded. Part of the measured "motor deficit" may therefore be attention or comorbidity rather than a pure motor deficit, but about half of children with ADHD still fall in the impaired range. Direct posture evidence in ADHD is thin and cross-sectional: more forward head posture, more postural sway, and different balance strategies. I could not search sleep and nutrition mediators before the search budget ran out.

### Cited Findings
- **Prevalence of motor deficit.**
  - Farran et al. 2020 (Advances in Neurodevelopmental Disorders; 51 children with ADHD aged 8–15 vs 75 motor-matched typically developing children): 47% of the ADHD sample had a motor deficit. The paper asks whether motor impairment is part of the ADHD phenotype or a co-occurring deficit; its conclusion could not be retrieved. [moderate] — [Farran et al. 2020, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41252-020-00159-6); [Goldsmiths eprint](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/28340/8/Farran2020_Article_IsTheMotorImpairmentInAttentio.pdf)
  - Across psychiatric conditions, the prevalence of motor impairment ranges from 8% to 73% depending on the tool and cut-off. [moderate] — [Antwerp systematic review record](https://repository.uantwerpen.be/link/irua/128628)
- **Attention as a correlate of motor scores.**
  - Piek et al. 2004: sustained attention and impulse control were good concurrent predictors of fine and gross motor skill in 6–11-year-olds with ADHD.
  - Piek et al. 1999: inattentive symptom severity predicted coordination difficulty. The inattentive presentation had poorer fine motor skill and the combined presentation poorer gross motor skill.
  - [moderate] — cited in [Farran et al. 2020](https://eprints-gro.gold.ac.uk/28340/1/Farran%20et%20al%20ADHDmotor%20ANDD2020.pdf) and [Fine and gross motor ability in males with ADHD, Curtin](https://espace.curtin.edu.au/handle/20.500.11937/21359?show=full)
- **Comorbidity confound.** A 2013 J Autism Dev Disord brief report (16 per group) was titled "Children with ADHD without co-morbid autism do not have impaired motor proficiency". Inattention, not hyperactivity, was associated with poorer motor performance, possibly via social-responsiveness impairment. The sample is tiny. [weak] — [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/brief-report-children-with-adhd-without-co-morbid-autism-do-not-/)
- **Posture.**
  - Naufal et al. 2026 (BIO Web of Conferences; 200 children aged 6–12; cross-sectional): ADHD score correlated with craniovertebral angle (r = −0.537), meaning more forward head posture. Proposed contributors were inattention, hyperactivity, retained primitive reflexes and screen use. [weak; conference paper, single study] — [BIO Web Conf 2026](https://www.bio-conferences.org/articles/bioconf/pdf/2026/10/bioconf_micssh2026_02007.pdf)
  - Christ et al. 2025 (Front Hum Neurosci; 17 ADHD vs 24 controls): children with ADHD used about 7.5–11% higher upper-body joint torques in static and dynamic balance tasks. [weak] — [Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2025, DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/07ac48b18d29404994560d8e6655f588)
  - Eur J Pediatr 2012 (24 ADHD vs 17 controls): greater mediolateral sway in single- and dual-task conditions. [weak] — [Eur J Pediatr 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00431-012-1695-7)
- **Sleep and nutrition.** No sources were retrieved; the search budget ran out. See Gaps.

### Inferences
- In this person, "poor sports ability" may combine a true coordination deficit (likely DCD-like), attention-dependent performance (worse in tests and coaching that need sustained attention and rule-following), and social or motivational barriers in team sport. [interpretation]
- The forward-head finding is the only direct ADHD–posture association found. It is cross-sectional and confounded by screen use, so it cannot show that ADHD shaped posture. [interpretation]
- Postural *control* (balance, sway) is better documented as impaired in ADHD than static postural *alignment*. Poor postural control could, in principle, lead to compensatory static habits. That idea is untested. [speculative]

### Gaps
- Sleep: ADHD is commonly associated with sleep problems. I could not retrieve or verify any meta-analysis (e.g., Díaz-Román 2018), nor any evidence that ADHD-related sleep problems affect growth, muscle or motor learning. Mark any such claim [speculative] until sourced.
- Nutrition (e.g., iron or vitamin D) as a mediator of motor or bone development in ADHD: not searched (budget exhausted).
- Effort, motivation or reward effects on strength and motor test scores in ADHD, e.g., studies giving rewards during motor tests: not found.
- The Farran 2020 conclusion (core phenotype vs comorbidity) was not retrieved.

## Q8. Which pathways have causal-inference-grade evidence, and which could plausibly have shaped this person's body?

### Takeaway
No pathway from ADHD to adult *posture* or *body shape* has causal-inference-grade evidence. The best-supported explanation for "ADHD plus lifelong clumsiness" is **shared genetic liability** with DCD (twin studies), which is co-occurrence, not causation. Perinatal factors (preterm birth, oxygen complications) are a common cause with quasi-causal support. A downstream behavioural cascade of low motor competence → less activity → lower fitness and bone loading → higher adiposity has moderate observational support. Stimulant effects on height are small. The ADHD → BMI causal effect is weak and contested.
