## 1. Strength development before vs after puberty: how much is neural vs muscular?

### Takeaway
Before puberty, children are weaker than adults even after body-size normalisation. A sizeable part of the gap appears to be neural: lower voluntary activation, slower rate of torque development, longer electromechanical delay, and more antagonist coactivation in complex tasks. Prepubertal resistance training produces real strength gains that are mostly attributed to neural and coordination adaptation rather than hypertrophy. Two cautions apply. The voluntary-activation evidence is inconsistent across studies and methodologically fragile, and the "children under-use type II motor units" hypothesis (Dotan 2012) is still unproven.

### Cited Findings
**Voluntary activation (VA) and the type II motor-unit hypothesis**
- Dotan, Mitchell, Cohen, Klentrou, Gabriel & Falk (2012), "Child–adult differences in muscle activation — a review," *Pediatric Exercise Science* 24(1):2–21. The review argues that children's lower activation is best explained by lower use of higher-threshold type II motor units. In the authors' words: "Although conclusive evidence is still lacking, only this hypothesis of differential motor-unit activation in children and adults appears capable of accounting for all observed child–adult differences." Other factors (dimensionality, intramuscular synchronisation, coactivation, muscle composition) "can explain some, but not all." [weak–moderate: narrative review, hypothesis-generating] (SS) — [record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4024653?lng=en); [author full text, not accessible this session](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/7722/2/Fulltext.pdf)
- Counterpoint: an O'Brien et al. commentary argues that claiming differential motor-unit activation *alone* explains child–adult differences overreaches, and that many other variables contribute. A second commentary calls measuring VA in children "a methodological challenge." [weak: commentary] (SS) — [search record list incl. HAL commentary](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01659903)
- Woods et al., "Increase in volitional muscle activation from childhood to adulthood: a systematic review and meta-analysis," *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 54(5):789–799. It was e-published Dec 2021 and appeared in the 2022 print volume (PMID 34967802). Children were 7–14 y and adults 16–28 y. Of 1,915 records, 19 datasets entered the qualitative synthesis and 14 the meta-analysis (207 children, 193 adults). Pooled **Hedges' g = 1.55 (CI 0.9–2.13)**, i.e. lower VA in children. Only **9/19 (47%)** studies individually found significantly lower VA in children. The review used the central activation ratio (VA_CAR), whose validity and reliability "have recently been questioned." The age effect may depend on the muscle tested (upper vs lower limb; upper-body data are limited). [strong for the direction; moderate for the magnitude, given heterogeneity and method concerns] (SS) — [Ovid full-text listing](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-msse/fulltext/10.1249/mss.0000000000002853~increase-in-volitional-muscle-activation-from-childhood-to)
- I could not confirm the publication-bias result the caller already holds (a funnel-plot or Egger finding) in any search summary. Treat it as from the caller's own reading.
- Single studies: "Children have a reduced maximal voluntary activation level of the adductor pollicis muscle compared to adults" (*Eur J Appl Physiol* 2015; title only seen) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-015-3132-x). A separate study examined the effect of muscle length on VA in children vs adults (title only) — [PubMed 25083726](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25083726/). [weak: findings not seen]

**Rate of torque development (RTD) and electromechanical delay (EMD)**
- Dotan et al. (2013), "Child–adult differences in the kinetics of torque development," *J Sports Sci* 31(9):945–953. 20 untrained boys (mean 10.1 y) were compared with 20 untrained men (22.9 y) in explosive isometric elbow flexion and knee extension. The boys' body-mass-normalised knee-extension MVC was **26% lower**, peak RTD **17% lower**, and %MVC reached at 100 ms **23% lower**; time to 30% and 80% MVC was longer. Elbow flexion showed similar or larger gaps. The authors argued this gap "cannot be explained by muscle-composition and/or musculo-tendinous-stiffness differences" and that it indirectly supports children's lower use of type II motor units. [moderate] (SS) — [record](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201312008240)
- Falk et al. (2009, *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab*): 15 boys vs 16 men; EMD **75.5 ± 28.4 vs 47.6 ± 17.5 ms** (longer in boys). Cohen et al. (2010), endurance-trained boys vs men: EMD 64.7 ± 17.1 vs 56.6 ± 15.4 ms; endurance training did not change strength or RTD in either group. [moderate] (SS) — [search source set incl. PMC3804465](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3804465); [Brock record](https://brocku.scholaris.ca/items/d791242d-5100-489e-ab70-6ea5af9af386)
- Method caveat from the same group: threshold-based torque-onset methods "can misrepresent reality and lead to erroneous conclusions when comparing children and adults." [moderate] (SS) — [LJMU repository](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/4255/)

**Antagonist coactivation**
- Systematic review (Woods, O'Mahoney, McKiel, Natale, Falk): 25 studies from 1,083 screened, healthy children vs young adults. Child–adult differences in coactivation are more common in **multi-joint dynamic** tasks, where coactivation is generally higher in children, and far less common in single-joint isometric tasks. [moderate–strong: systematic review, no pooled effect seen] (SS) — [Brock University](https://brocku.scholaris.ca/items/42efcb54-b5bb-49d4-be60-e52d2c8be018)
- Hassani et al.: prepubertal boys vs men at three knee angles; antagonist activity was higher in children at all angles. [weak–moderate] (SS) — [AKUT](https://ojs.utlib.ee/index.php/AKUT/article/view/akut.2009.14.02)
- Counter-evidence, O'Brien et al. (men, women, boys, girls): adults had greater *absolute* antagonist coactivation, but relative to agonist moment there was no group difference (all groups **7–8%**). [moderate] (SS) — [Springer EJAP 2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-009-1088-4)
- A 1999 study of pubertal children vs adults found moment per unit agonist EMG and antagonist activity similar, and not sex-dependent. [weak] (SS) — [Springer EJAP 1999](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s004210050545)

**Training in prepubertal children: gains without hypertrophy?**
- Ramsay, Blimkie et al. (1990), *Med Sci Sports Exerc*. 13 boys aged 9–11 trained and 13 served as controls; progressive resistance training 3×/week for 20 weeks. 1RM bench press rose **+35%** and leg press **+22%**; evoked twitch torque of elbow flexors and knee extensors rose **+30%** each. Percent motor-unit activation showed only a *trend* upward. The authors credited improved motor-skill coordination (especially early) and a tendency toward increased motor-unit activation. I could not see the primary cross-sectional-area result; secondary sources say training had "little if any effect on muscle size" in preadolescents. [moderate for the strength gain; weak for "no hypertrophy" as stated here] (SS) — [PubMed 2233199](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2233199/); [Blimkie 1993 review, Sports Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.2165/00007256-199315060-00004)
- A review states that it remains "unclear whether children's improvements in muscle strength are accompanied by increases in muscle size": one prepubertal study found no CSA change, another found significant increases. [weak–moderate] (SS) — [JSSM 2007 review](https://www.jssm.org/06-3-353.p_d_f)
- Behringer et al. (2010), *Pediatrics* meta-analysis of resistance training in children and adolescents: pooled **ES = 1.12**. Strength trainability rises with age and maturity, but "there is no noticeable boost during puberty." Longer programmes and more sets gave larger gains. [strong for the effect; moderate for moderators] (SS) — [SPONET record](https://sponet.de/Record/4020373); [HealthDay summary](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/exercise-and-fitness/strength-training-generally-effective-in-children-644794.html)

### Inferences
- [speculative] The prepubertal strength gap has a substantial neural component: lower and more variable VA, slower RTD, longer EMD, and more coactivation in complex tasks. This is the layer most plausibly affected by a neurodevelopmental condition like ADHD. The post-pubertal jump in absolute strength is classically attributed to androgen-driven hypertrophy. The Behringer finding of *no* puberty boost in *trainability* suggests that "hormones" and "neural" are not cleanly separable stages.
- [speculative] For the person in this report: if motor-unit recruitment, coordination, or coactivation patterns were immature or deviant in childhood, the literature predicts weaker, slower and less efficient force production. It does not by itself predict a permanent structural change in muscle.
- [speculative] Coactivation is higher in children mainly in *multi-joint dynamic* tasks, which is what sports skills are. That fits a story of "clumsy, stiff" movement more than one of "weak isolated muscle."

### Gaps
- Original texts of Woods (MSSE) and Dotan (2012) could not be opened. I could not verify the publication-bias test, per-muscle subgroup values, or the I² value.
- Unverified prior knowledge, check before use: Ozmun, Mikesky & Surburg (1994, *Med Sci Sports Exerc*) reported about +28% strength with about +17% integrated EMG and no change in arm anthropometry after 8 weeks of training in prepubescent children. This is a classic "neural adaptation" citation.
- Unverified prior knowledge: in boys, peak strength velocity typically follows peak height velocity by about 0.5–1.5 years (Beunen & Malina). Not checked this session.
- I found no study measuring VA, RTD or coactivation in children *with ADHD* specifically, nor any spinal reciprocal-inhibition data in ADHD.

## 2. Corticospinal tract (CST) maturation: TMS thresholds, latency, CMCT, ipsilateral projections, SICI, transcallosal inhibition, DTI myelination

### Takeaway
Fast corticospinal *conduction* matures early. Central motor conduction time (CMCT) reaches adult values in childhood, with reported ages ranging from about 2 y (central delay) to about 13 y (CMCT). Cortical *excitability* (motor threshold), *inhibitory* intracortical circuits (SICI) and *transcallosal inhibition* mature much later: SICI is reported lower in children and still immature in adolescence, and transcallosal inhibition is reported absent in preschoolers. Ipsilateral CST projections are pruned by activity-dependent withdrawal from about 3–18 months. Motor *performance* keeps improving into adulthood after the fast CST is mature. DTI shows CST and corpus callosum microstructure continuing to develop through adolescence.

### Cited Findings
**Conduction (CMCT, MEP latency)**
- Koh & Eyre (1988), *Arch Dis Child* 63(11):1347–1352. A TMS developmental study using height ÷ cortical-to-muscle latency as an index of descending conduction velocity. [moderate; numbers not seen] (SS) — [ADC](https://adc.bmj.com/content/63/11/1347)
- Eyre, Miller & Ramesh (1991): central motor and somatosensory conduction delays "rapidly decrease over the first 2 years after birth and thereafter remain constant at adult values." Peripheral delays fall at first, then from about 5 y increase in proportion to arm length. [moderate] (SS) — [search source set](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1181427)
- Fietzek et al. (2000), *Dev Med Child Neurol* 42(4):220–227: 112 participants aged 0.2–30 y. CMCTs under facilitation vs relaxation reach adult values at *different* ages, explained by maturation of cortical and spinal excitability. A "stable phase for CMCTs and reaction time was reached during childhood," while motor speed and skill (finger tapping, diadochokinesis, tracking, ballistic velocity) "continued into adulthood." The authors' conclusion: "the maturation of the fast CS tract seems to be completed before the acquisition of the related motor performance has been accomplished." [moderate, cross-sectional] (SS) — [PubMed 10795559](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10795559/)
- Korean pediatric MEP study, healthy children aged 1–19: first-dorsal-interosseous MEPs were obtainable from **13 months**. The frequency of obtaining MEPs rose with age. Amplitudes increased with age while latencies stayed relatively constant (height-compensated), and **CMCT reached adult values by about 13 y**. [weak–moderate] (SS) — [KoreaMed Synapse](https://synapse.koreamed.org/articles/1149466)
- Turkish normative study (2021), 46 healthy participants aged 3–20.5 y: MEP latency correlated with age (**r = 0.69**, P < 0.001) and height (**r = 0.80**). Unresponsiveness was significantly higher **below 7 y**. [weak: n = 46] (SS) — [Ege University repository](https://acikerisim.ege.edu.tr/items/89f7c475-d5ae-4c96-a9ef-4db4195abe04)
- Heinen and colleagues, children aged 6–9 vs adults: CMCT under facilitation and the post-excitatory silent period were similar to adults. CMCT at rest, the "latency jump" (rest minus facilitated latency) and required stimulus intensity differed. In other words, conduction is adult-like by 6–9 y, but resting excitability is not. [weak] (SS) — [search source set](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC488249)
- A review describes TMS-measured maturation as "a progressive increment of cortical excitability from infancy to adulthood that completes in mid-adolescence." [weak: secondary] (SS) — [2019 CP/TMS-DTI paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6668599)
- A 2023 *Scientific Reports* paper models MEP features across age groups from childhood to adulthood, reporting overlapping timing features between children and preadolescents. Detail not seen. [weak] (SS) — [Nature Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-37775-w)

**Ipsilateral projections**
- Eyre et al. (2001), *Neurology* 57:1543–1554: 9 neonates followed longitudinally for 2 y, plus 85 healthy subjects from birth to adulthood. Ipsilateral and contralateral responses diverge from **3 months**. By **18 months**, ipsilateral responses are smaller, need higher thresholds and have longer latencies. The authors interpret this as activity-dependent withdrawal of ipsilateral corticospinal axons. [moderate–strong for infancy] (SS) — [Aberystwyth record](https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/evidence-of-activity-dependent-withdrawal-of-corticospinal-projec/)
- A review states that the likelihood of ipsilateral MEPs "is markedly decreased after approximately 6 months of age" in typical development. [weak] (SS) — [PMC3679902](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3679902)
- An abstract of a study of 50 normal children aged 3–11 y (identification uncertain; probably Müller, Kass-Iliyya & Reitz, *Ann Neurol* 1997;42:705–711) found ipsilateral responses in about **two-thirds** of children, more often in proximal than distal muscles. [weak–moderate] (SS) — [ScienceOpen abstract](https://www.scienceopen.com/document/vid/920a4340-e78f-486c-999d-1a12e749a48c); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ana.410420506)

**Intracortical inhibition (SICI)**
- Mall et al. (2004), *Neuropediatrics* 35(2):120–125, "Low level of intracortical inhibition in children shown by TMS": paired-pulse at 2 ms in children, adolescents and adults. [moderate–weak] (SS) — [Thieme](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-2004-815834)
- Walther et al. (2009), *Brain & Development*: 30 healthy participants aged 6–30 y, 5 interstimulus intervals. SICI was significantly lower in children than adults, and adolescents still showed less SICI at 5 ms than adults. **ICF showed no age difference.** [weak: n = 30] (SS) — [TUM portal](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/maturation-of-inhibitory-and-excitatory-motor-cortex-pathways-in-/)
- Counter-evidence: a 2014 *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* study states that research on early age-related differences in SICI and ICF "is also inconclusive," citing Mall 2004 among others. [weak] (SS) — [Frontiers 2014, 8:669](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2014.00669/full)

**Transcallosal inhibition**
- Heinen, Glocker, Fietzek, Meyer, Lücking & Korinthenberg (1998), *Ann Neurol* 43:608–612, "Absence of transcallosal inhibition following focal magnetic stimulation in pre-school children." The title states the finding; I did not see the ages or n. [moderate–weak] (SS) — [cited in Klin Neurophysiol 2004 review](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/s-2003-814838.pdf)
- A 2017 review links persistent childhood mirror movements to "immaturity of transcallosal inhibitory fibers," citing Heinen 1998. Ciechanski et al. (2017, *J Neurophysiol*) measured the ipsilateral silent period and paired-pulse interhemispheric inhibition in 19 typically developing right-handed young people and found sex effects, possibly reflecting different developmental timing in boys and girls. [weak] (SS) — [PMC5494372](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494372)

**DTI / myelination**
- Lebel et al. (2008), *NeuroImage* 40(3):1044–1055: DTI in 202 healthy people aged 5–30 y. FA follows an exponential-type maturation curve that plateaus in adolescence or early adulthood depending on region. [strong design; tract-specific plateau ages not seen] (SS) — [ScienceDirect](https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1053811907011779)
- Lebel & Beaulieu (2011), *J Neurosci* 31(30):10937, "Longitudinal development of human brain wiring continues from childhood into adulthood." Title only seen. [strong design] — [J Neurosci](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/31/30/10937)
- Central and posterior white matter plateaus earlier than peripheral or anterior white matter; frontotemporal white matter keeps developing into early adulthood. [moderate] (SS) — [Frontiers Neuroanat 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroanatomy/articles/10.3389/fnana.2016.00009/full)
- Tractography shows maturation of internal capsule, corpus callosum, **CST** and uncinate integrity "that continue through childhood and adolescence." [moderate] (SS) — [Asato et al. 2010, "White matter development in adolescence: a DTI study"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2923214/)

### Inferences
- [moderate inference] A hierarchy emerges: (1) the fast conduction "cable" matures early (CMCT adult-like somewhere between about 2 and 13 y depending on method); (2) the excitatory threshold falls gradually through childhood to mid-adolescence; (3) inhibitory tuning (SICI, transcallosal inhibition) is the last to mature, still immature in adolescence. The typical child is therefore a "low-brake" system. That makes "reduced brake" in ADHD hard to separate from "younger-looking brake."
- [speculative] Motor *performance* lags behind CST conduction maturity (Fietzek). The bottleneck for skilled movement in mid-childhood is therefore not conduction speed but control (inhibition, timing, cerebellar calibration).

### Gaps
- Exact age curves for resting motor threshold (e.g. %MSO by age), SICI by age and iSP latency/duration by age were not retrievable. No large normative TMS dataset (n > 100) was found.
- Unverified prior knowledge: Garvey et al. (2003, *Clin Neurophysiol* 114:1662–1670), "Cortical correlates of neuromotor development in healthy children," reported that iSP latency and duration mature with age in parallel with the decline in mirror movements, and that motor threshold falls with age. I could not retrieve it.
- Unverified prior knowledge: Lebel 2008 reportedly found the fornix and corpus callosum (genu/splenium) among the earliest to reach about 90% of adult FA, and the cingulum and uncinate the latest. Not verified.
- Unverified prior knowledge: on conventional clinical T1/T2 MRI, myelination looks essentially adult-like by about 2–3 y. A routine MRI at age 9 therefore cannot show "delayed myelination" in the way DTI research can. Check a pediatric neuroradiology source.

## 3. Motor overflow / mirror movements: typical decline and neural basis

### Takeaway
Mirror movements are normal in young children. They decline steeply from about 5–8 y, are usually gone by about 10 y and largely absent by adolescence, then rise slightly again in older adulthood. The decline is attributed to maturing inhibition: transcallosal inhibition and possibly SICI, plus withdrawal of ipsilateral projections. Children with ADHD show more overflow and less age-related decline.

### Cited Findings
- Review statement: mirror movements are present in typically developing children, "decrease gradually between 5 years and 8 years of age and have usually disappeared by 10 years of age." [weak–moderate: secondary] (SS) — [CP reliability paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348620642_Assessment_of_mirror_movements_in_children_and_adolescents_with_unilateral_cerebral_palsy_reliability_of_the_Woods_and_Teuber_scale); [Koerte 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21039436/)
- Koerte et al. (2010), *Dev Med Child Neurol*: healthy participants aged **3–96 y**. Mirror movements are a "well-known transient phenomenon in children under the age of 10." The mirror-movement ratio declines steeply through childhood and adolescence and rises gradually in adulthood. The decline is "suggested to be an effect of the maturation of the motor system owing to an increase in inhibitory competence." [moderate] (SS) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-8749.2010.03766.x)
- Overflow frequency and severity decrease with age and are "largely absent by the time typically developing children reach adolescence." [moderate] (SS) — [Ojuri et al. 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cns3.20100)
- Ojuri et al. (2025), *Annals of the Child Neurology Society*, quantified mirror overflow in ADHD vs typically developing youth aged 8–18. TD youth showed a moderate decrease with age on the "TOF" task, a large decrease for left-hand finger tapping, and no significant decrease for right-hand finger tapping. Children with ADHD show more persistent overflow. [moderate] (SS) — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cns3.20100)
- fMRI study title: "Motor overflow in children with ADHD is associated with decreased extent of neural activation in the motor cortex." Title only seen. [weak–moderate] — [PMC4554770](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4554770/)
- Mechanism links: transcallosal inhibition is absent in preschoolers (Heinen 1998, §2), and ipsilateral projections are withdrawn in infancy but partly present at 3–11 y (§2). [moderate–weak] — [PMC5494372](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5494372)

### Inferences
- [moderate inference] Mirror overflow is the most *visible* clinical marker of the "brake" maturing. Its timetable (gone by about 10 y) is a useful anchor: in the report's subject, the age-9 period sits right at the end of the normal overflow window.
- [speculative] Excess overflow and coactivation would make fast, isolated, dissociated movements (dribbling, racket skills, fine finger work) harder to learn. That fits a history of "very poor at sports and slow to learn movements."

### Gaps
- Unverified prior knowledge: Mostofsky and colleagues (Kennedy Krieger), e.g. MacNeil et al. (2011, *Neuropsychology*?), "Quantitative assessment of motor overflow in children with ADHD." Not retrieved.
- No study found that ties childhood mirror-overflow severity to *adult* posture or body shape.

## 4. Cerebellum and motor learning in typical children

### Takeaway
The cerebellum matures late, following an inverted-U volume trajectory with maturation continuing into late adolescence; Tiemeier 2010 is the key longitudinal study. I could not retrieve specific peak ages or developmental data on cerebellar motor learning in this session.

### Cited Findings
- Tiemeier et al. (2010), *NeuroImage* 49(1):63–70: longitudinal MRI of total cerebellum plus 11 subdivisions, ages about 5–24, scanned 3 times at 2-year intervals, testing sex differences. [strong design] (SS) — [PMC2775156](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2775156); [Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/28347); [design table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7197862)
- A secondary citation describes cerebellar maturation as continuing into late adolescence with an inverted-U shape. [weak: secondary] (SS) — [PMC7927303](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7927303)
- Motor speed and skill keep developing into adulthood (Fietzek 2000, §2). Largo et al. (2001), "Neuromotor development from 5 to 18 years. Part 1: timed performance" (*DMCN*), is the standard Swiss normative source; title only seen. [moderate] — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1469-8749.2001.tb00739.x)

### Inferences
- [speculative] The cerebellum matures later than the CST cable, and it supports motor-skill calibration and timing. Its maturation window therefore overlaps the ages when sports skills are normally acquired (about 6–15 y).

### Gaps
- Unverified prior knowledge: Tiemeier 2010 reportedly found total cerebellar volume peaking around **11.8 y in girls and 15.6 y in boys**. Verify before use.
- Developmental motor-adaptation and sequence-learning data (e.g. locomotor split-belt adaptation in children; implicit sequence learning fMRI) were not retrieved.
- Typical motor milestones in infancy (WHO Motor Development Study windows) and fundamental movement skill norms were not retrieved in this session.

## 5. ADHD structural brain development: cortex, subcortex, cerebellum, corpus callosum, DTI of CST. Delay or deviance?

### Takeaway
At the group level, ADHD cortex follows the normal shape of development, delayed by about 3 years in cortical thickness peak (Shaw 2007). The **primary motor cortex is the single exception: it matures earlier.** ENIGMA mega-analyses show small volume and area reductions (d about −0.1 to −0.2) that are largest in children and absent in adults, consistent with "delay." Against a pure delay model: cerebellar (superior vermis) reductions look fixed and non-progressive; corpus callosum (splenium) reductions are reported but not known to persist; CST DTI findings are inconsistent even in direction; and population-based ABCD effects are smaller still. The best summary is "mostly delay, with some fixed deviance (cerebellum) and a motor-cortex exception." Group effects are far too small to diagnose or explain an individual.

### Cited Findings
**Cortical maturation (NIMH, Shaw et al. 2007, *PNAS*)**
- 223 youth with ADHD vs matched controls (446 total), most scanned at least twice about 3 years apart; peak cortical thickness timing estimated at about 40,000 cortical points. Median age at which half the cortex reached peak thickness: **10.5 y in ADHD vs 7.5 y in controls**. The lag was largest in prefrontal regions for attention and planning, and the pattern was "normal but delayed." [strong: longitudinal, large] (SS) — [NIMH release](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/news/science-news/2007/brain-matures-a-few-years-late-in-adhd-but-follows-normal-pattern); [NIH release](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/brain-matures-few-years-late-adhd-follows-normal-pattern); [PMC2148352](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2148352)
- **The primary motor cortex was the only area that matured faster than normal in ADHD.** The authors suggested that early motor maturation combined with late frontal control might explain restlessness and fidgeting. A companion commentary by Rubia called the study direct evidence for the maturational-delay hypothesis. [strong for the observation; speculative for the interpretation] (SS) — [NIMH release](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/news/science-updates/2007/brain-matures-a-few-years-late-in-adhd-but-follows-normal-pattern); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/11/071112172200.htm)
- Shaw's own caveat: "Brain imaging is still not ready for use as a diagnostic tool in ADHD." — [NIH release](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/brain-matures-few-years-late-adhd-follows-normal-pattern)

**ENIGMA-ADHD (mega-analyses)**
- Hoogman et al. (2017), *Lancet Psychiatry* 4(4):310–319: 1,713 ADHD vs 1,529 controls, 23 sites, ages 4–63, 66% male. Small reductions, e.g. **accumbens d = −0.15, amygdala d = −0.19**. **Effects were largest in children and absent in adults.** There was no effect of stimulant medication or comorbidity and no correlation with symptom scores. The authors suggested "a delay of maturation and a delay of degeneration" (exploratory modelling). [strong] (SS) — [VU Amsterdam record](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/subcortical-brain-volume-differences-in-participants-with-attenti/); [PMC5933934](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5933934)
- Hoogman et al. (2019), *Am J Psychiatry*: in children, the largest effect was lower **total cortical surface area, d = −0.21 (95% CI −0.29 to −0.12)** (1,081 ADHD / 1,048 controls), mainly frontal, cingulate and temporal. Fusiform and temporal-pole thickness were also lower in children. **No surface-area or thickness differences in adolescents or adults.** [strong] (SS) — [AJP](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2019.18091033)
- ABCD (Bernanke et al. 2022, *Lancet Psychiatry*), US population-based sample of 9–10-year-olds: effects were smaller and fewer than in ENIGMA. ENIGMA ranges are summarised as d −0.19 to −0.13 (subcortical) and −0.21 to −0.10 (cortical). [strong] (SS) — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2215036621005058)

**Cerebellum**
- Castellanos et al. (2002), *JAMA*, longitudinal NIMH cohort: a "fixed, non-progressive reduction in total cerebellar volume," among the most persistent differences in both boys and girls. [strong: longitudinal] (SS) — [review summary](https://austinpublishinggroup.com/psychiatry-behavioral-sciences/fulltext/ajpbs-v1-id1013.php)
- Mackie et al. (2007), *Am J Psychiatry* 164:647, 36 children with ADHD from that cohort: **superior vermis** volume loss persisted regardless of clinical outcome ("fixed, nonprogressive" substrate). Children with **worse outcomes** showed a downward trajectory in right and left **inferior-posterior cerebellar lobes**. [moderate] (SS) — [AJP](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/ajp.2007.164.4.647)
- Counter-evidence: a 2025 study notes "mixed results regarding the cerebellar volume in young patients with ADHD." A multicohort longitudinal study of cerebellar development in ADHD exists (title only seen). [moderate] (SS) — [JCPP 2025](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jcpp.70072); [UMC Utrecht record](https://researchinformation.umcutrecht.nl/en/publications/a-multicohort-longitudinal-study-of-cerebellar-development-in-att/)

**Corpus callosum**
- Hutchinson, Mathias & Banich (2008), *Neuropsychology* 22(3):341–349, meta-analysis of 13 studies: the **splenium was smaller** in children and adolescents with ADHD, driven mainly by **females**; boys showed a smaller **rostral body**. Comorbidity was inconsistently reported, and **it is unknown whether differences persist into adulthood.** Valera et al. (2006/2007) had also reported a splenial reduction. [moderate–strong] (SS) — [CU Boulder record](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_11498)
- Counter-evidence: other studies found no splenial abnormality, or differences in other segments. [moderate] (SS) — [Adelaide thesis review](https://thesis.library.adelaide.edu.au/dspace/handle/2440/51097)
- Ameis et al. (2016), *Am J Psychiatry*, DTI in children with ADHD, ASD, OCD and controls: **lower splenium FA in every disorder group**, i.e. a shared rather than ADHD-specific finding. FA was lower in ADHD than OCD in several tracts including the CST. [moderate] (SS) — [AJP](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15111435)

**CST microstructure (DTI)**
- Inattentive-presentation ADHD: **higher** FA in the left CST vs neurotypical children. [weak–moderate] (SS) — [PMC5663221](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5663221)
- Drug-naive children with ADHD: altered CST along-tract measures, related to attention and response inhibition. The introduction cites a DTI meta-analysis (mostly pediatric) with abnormalities in the anterior corona radiata and internal capsule, which the CST passes through. [moderate] (SS) — [J Psychiatry Neurosci](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/10.1503/jpn.190024)
- No CST-specific meta-analysis was found, and single-study results conflict in direction. [gap noted by search]

### Inferences
- [moderate inference] **Delay vs deviance:** cortical thickness, surface area and subcortical volumes support *delay*: same shape, later peak, case–control differences vanishing by adulthood. The cerebellar vermis supports *fixed deviance*. Primary motor cortex is *deviant in the opposite direction* (early). The corpus callosum and CST are unresolved.
- [speculative] Early motor-cortex maturation alongside late frontal, cerebellar and callosal control is the structural analogue of the "accelerator early, brake late" hypothesis. Cortical thickness maturation is not the same thing as corticospinal excitability, though; the bridge is inferential.
- [inference, statistical] A d of about 0.2 means the ADHD and control distributions overlap by roughly 90% or more. These findings cannot be used to say that any one person's brain was "underdeveloped."
- [speculative] Because ENIGMA differences are absent in adults, an adult MRI today would not be expected to show residual structural signs. Any long-term effect on posture would have to run through *behaviour and learning history* (less practice, avoidance of sports, habitual postures) rather than through persisting brain-structure differences.

### Gaps
- Unverified prior knowledge, from the Hoogman 2017 abstract: caudate d about −0.11, hippocampus about −0.11, putamen about −0.14, ICV about −0.10; pallidum and thalamus not significant. Verify.
- Unverified prior knowledge: Castellanos 2002 included about 152 ADHD and 139 controls, with total cerebral volume about 3–4% smaller and parallel (non-converging) trajectories. Verify.
- Unverified prior knowledge: Shaw et al. (2012, *Biol Psychiatry*) reported delayed surface-area development (about 2 years) in ADHD. Not retrieved.
- Unverified prior knowledge: Valera et al. (2007, *Biol Psychiatry*) is the structural meta-analysis showing cerebellar regions among the largest reductions.
- Basal ganglia and dopamine as they bear on motor control (caudate/putamen trajectories, DAT imaging, timing deficits) were not retrieved this session.
- Normative-modelling or "brain-age gap" studies on delay vs deviance (e.g. Wolfers et al.) were not retrieved.

## 6. ADHD motor-cortex physiology (TMS), methylphenidate, and the "accelerator normal/early, brake lagging" hypothesis

### Takeaway
The most replicated physiological finding in ADHD is **reduced SICI** (GABA-A intracortical inhibition). The 2025 meta-analysis of 17 studies gives SMD 0.65, and the reduction correlates with symptom severity and modestly with motor-development scores. At the same time, **excitatory measures are not different**: motor thresholds, MEP amplitude and ICF do not differ. That pattern supports a "normal accelerator, weak brake" reading. Methylphenidate acutely and sub-chronically restores SICI. Counter-evidence: (a) task-related *up-modulation* of excitability is also reduced, so the accelerator's *dynamic* control is impaired too; (b) transcallosal inhibition (iSP) is not different in the meta-analysis, and one large study found only slower latency; (c) reduced SICI is non-specific and only moderately reliable; (d) because SICI is naturally low in typical children, "ADHD SICI" may simply look younger. Nothing shows the accelerator is *early*, and spinal reciprocal-inhibition data in ADHD were not found.

### Cited Findings
**SICI reduced; excitation unchanged**
- Han et al. (2025), *Front Psychiatry*, DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1544816: meta-analysis of cortical excitability across 17 studies. **SICI was reduced in ADHD: pooled SMD = 0.65 (95% CI 0.41–0.88, P < 0.00001).** There were **no significant differences** in MEP, aMT/rMT, cSP, **iSP**, or ICF. Reduced SICI is a candidate marker, not an established biomarker. [strong for SICI; age mix of included studies not seen] (SS) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1544816/full); [PMC11865255](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11865255/)
- An earlier meta-analysis (2016, *Res Dev Disabil*, PMID 27240241) of 9 studies also found reduced SICI, with the authors urging caution given the small number of studies. [moderate] (SS) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27240241/)
- Moll et al. (2000), *Neurosci Lett*: 18 drug-naive children with ADHD aged 8–12 vs 18 controls. **Motor threshold, cSP and ICF did not differ; intracortical inhibition was reduced.** A single **10 mg methylphenidate** dose significantly increased inhibition. [weak–moderate: small n] (SS) — [Göttingen record](https://publications.goettingen-research-online.de/ID-17114)
- Buchmann et al. (2007), *Biol Psychiatry*, "Restoration of disturbed intracortical motor inhibition and facilitation in ADHD children by methylphenidate": 18 MPH-naive children, mean age 11, treated 10–14 days. MPH normalised SICI, **LICI and ICF**; the inhibitory changes tracked clinical improvement. [weak–moderate] (SS) — [PubMed 17630869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17630869); [Cochrane table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6494554/table/table-wrap78)
  - Conflict: Buchmann describes *facilitation* as disturbed, while Moll and the 2025 meta-analysis found ICF unchanged. The excitatory side is therefore not uniformly normal.
- Gilbert et al. (2011), *Neurology*: 49 ADHD vs 49 TD, ages 8–12. **SICI 40% weaker** in ADHD (p < 0.0001). Weaker SICI correlated with ADHD severity (**r = −0.52**, p = 0.002). PANESS motor-development scores were **59% worse** in ADHD, and worse PANESS correlated modestly with weaker SICI (**r = −0.30**, p = 0.01). [moderate] (SS) — [PubMed 21321335](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21321335)
- Hoegl et al. (2012), *PLoS ONE*: in children with ADHD and high hyperactivity/impulsivity, SICI was markedly reduced at rest and during response preparation. [weak–moderate] (SS) — [PMC3457950](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3457950/)

**The accelerator's dynamic control is also impaired (counter-evidence)**
- Gilbert et al. (2019), *Neurology*, DOI 10.1212/WNL.0000000000007899: 66 ADHD vs 65 TD, right-handed, ages 8–12, TMS during a stop-signal task. Less SICI at rest and during go and stop trials, **and less task-related up-modulation (TRUM)** of MEPs from rest to action selection. Stop-signal performance was comparable between groups. [moderate] (SS) — [Neurology](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000007899); [AAN press release](https://www.aan.com/PressRoom/Home/PressRelease/2736)
- A follow-up Gilbert-group abstract using lower-intensity TMS found TRUM **33% lower** in ADHD (p = 0.075, a trend), significant only for inhibition trials. [weak: conference abstract] (SS) — [ICNA 2024 e-poster](https://icnapedia.org/eposters/2024-239)

**Transcallosal / interhemispheric inhibition**
- Wu et al. (2012), *Pediatr Neurol*: 114 right-handed children aged 8–12. Mean **iSP latency was 5 ms longer** in ADHD (p = 0.007), read as slower interhemispheric inhibitory signalling. [moderate] (SS) — [PMC3418513](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3418513/)
- Garvey et al. (2005), *Clin Neurophysiol* 116(8):1889–1896, "The ipsilateral silent period in boys with ADHD." Citation confirmed; results not seen. — [GWU record](https://hsrc.himmelfarb.gwu.edu/smhs_psych_facpubs/821)
- Counter-evidence: the 2025 meta-analysis found **no significant iSP difference**. [strong-ish] (SS) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1544816/full)

**Reliability, specificity, mechanism**
- SICI test–retest over 3 weeks in 64 children aged 8–12 (ADHD and TD): **moderate** reliability, higher when single-pulse MEPs exceeded 0.25 mV. [moderate] (SS) — [PMC12250949](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12250949/)
- Reduced SICI "is not specific for ADHD"; it also occurs in other neurological and psychiatric disorders. [moderate] (SS) — [search summary with Han 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11865255/)
- Harris, Gilbert et al. (2021), *Clin Neurophysiol*: children with *higher* motor-cortex GABA+ (MRS) were *less* responsive to SICI. MRS-measured GABA and TMS-measured SICI are therefore not the same "brake." [weak–moderate] (SS) — [PMC8106665](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8106665)
- Atomoxetine (Gilbert group, 2014, *J Child Neurol*): after 4 weeks, "mean SICI was reduced 31.9% in responders and increased 6.1% in non-responders." Baseline SICI did not predict response, but change in SICI tracked symptom change. **The sign convention (ratio vs inhibition strength) is ambiguous in the summary; check the original.** [weak] (SS) — [PMC4092054](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4092054)

### Inferences
- **Evidence FOR "accelerator normal, brake weak":** [moderate–strong] SICI is reduced (meta SMD 0.65) while MT, MEP and ICF are unchanged (2025 meta-analysis; Moll 2000). Mirror overflow is excessive and persistent (§3). SICI relates to PANESS motor scores (r = −0.30). Shaw found M1 maturing early (structural, not physiological).
- **Evidence AGAINST, or complicating it:**
  - (1) "Early accelerator" is unsupported physiologically. If excitatory drive were maturing early, motor thresholds in children with ADHD should be *lower* than in age peers (MT normally falls with age), but meta-analytic MT shows no difference. [moderate inference]
  - (2) TRUM is reduced, so the system fails to *ramp up* excitation for action as well as to inhibit. "Accelerator" control is impaired too. [moderate]
  - (3) Transcallosal inhibition shows mixed results: latency slower in one study (n = 114), no iSP difference in the meta-analysis. [moderate]
  - (4) Typical children also have low SICI (§2), so reduced SICI in ADHD may mean "delayed brake" rather than "broken brake." No longitudinal TMS study was found that could separate the two. [speculative]
  - (5) Spinal reciprocal inhibition, the third "brake," has no ADHD data in what I found. [gap]
- [speculative] Better phrasing for the report: "In ADHD, the excitatory output of the motor cortex appears normal at rest, but GABA-A-mediated intracortical inhibition is weaker, and the ability to modulate excitability up and down for a task is also reduced. This resembles a younger motor cortex and coincides with persistent motor overflow."

### Gaps
- No longitudinal TMS study in ADHD across adolescence into adulthood was found, so whether reduced SICI normalises (delay) or persists (deviance) is unknown. How many of the 17 studies in the 2025 meta-analysis were in adults was not visible.
- Garvey 2005 iSP results in boys with ADHD were not retrieved.
- No study linking TMS inhibition measures in childhood ADHD to adult posture or musculoskeletal outcomes was found.

## 7. What could an age-9 report of "slow hand development" have been? Brain MRI vs hand-wrist bone-age X-ray

### Takeaway
The more plausible source of "slow hand development" is a **hand-wrist X-ray for bone age** (골연령/뼈나이 검사, often called "성장판 검사" colloquially in Korea), reported as "bone age delayed." It is the most common imaging test in pediatric growth workups, and it images the *hand*. A clinical brain MRI at 9 y would not normally grade "hand development." The "hand knob" is a recognised anatomical landmark of the motor hand area, used to localise lesions and for surgical planning, not a developmental measure. Delayed bone age means skeletal maturation behind chronological age, with remaining growth potential. Its causes include constitutional delay (familial late bloomers), nutrition, hormonal and chronic conditions. Recent data do *not* show ADHD medication delaying bone age or puberty, though stimulants cause modest height-velocity suppression.

### Cited Findings
**Bone age (hand-wrist X-ray)**
- Bone age measures skeletal maturity in growing children and can be used for adult-height prediction. X-ray is the most commonly used modality (CT/MRI possible). Atlas methods: Greulich–Pyle, Tanner–Whitehouse. [moderate: review] (SS) — [Bone-age mini-review, PMC7994346](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7994346/)
- Children of the same age vary in bone maturity "due to genetics, nutrition, hormonal factors, or underlying medical conditions." Height predictions from bone age "do not apply to children with endocrine or bone pathologies affecting growth." [moderate] (SS) — [PubMed 20190534](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20190534/); [PMC12025690](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12025690/)
- In Korea, bone-age testing is a 선택비급여 (selectively non-covered) item and was flagged for reassessment because of high public interest. This reflects how common it is in growth clinics. [moderate: NECA, national HTA agency] (SS) — [NECA ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.bwise.kr/neca/handle/2023.sw.neca/247?mode=full)
- Korean consumer Q&A: certain conditions can delay bone age. [weak] (SS) — [닥터나우 Q&A](https://doctornow.co.kr/content/qna/396b91c6905d11ed95e206ef18b6ac0c)

**ADHD, stimulants, and bone age / puberty / growth**
- ADDUCE project, Carucci et al. (2024), *J Atten Disord* (PMID 38366816): methylphenidate-treated and untreated ADHD groups did **not** differ in Tanner stages (no higher risk of pubertal delay). In a bone-age subset of **only 39** patients, bone maturation **accelerated slightly** after 24 months while predicted adult height stayed stable. The authors saw no safety concern for puberty or growth. [moderate; bone-age arm weak because n = 39] (SS) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38366816/); [Dundee PDF](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/files/120650906/Carucci_ADDUCE_pubertal_maturation_and_bone_age.pdf)
- Cervical vertebral and dental maturation in ADHD, with and without MPH: after age and sex adjustment, **no significant delay** vs controls. [weak–moderate] (SS) — [PubMed 30175189](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30175189)
- MTA study as summarised in a review: medicated children showed **0.9 cm/yr** growth suppression over 14 months and a further **1.03 cm/yr** over a 10-month follow-up, relative to peers. Bone-density data are mixed (one DXA study lower; Lahat 2000 found no effect on BMD turnover over 1–2 y). [moderate] (SS) — [Children 2022 review](https://doi.org/10.3390/children9091276); [Lahat et al. 2000, J Child Neurol](https://doi.org/10.1177/088307380001500702)

**Brain MRI and the "hand knob"**
- Yousry et al. (1997, *Brain*): the motor hand area localises to a knob on the precentral gyrus, omega- or epsilon-shaped on axial images and hook-shaped on sagittal images. It sits at the "middle knee" of the central sulcus, near where the superior frontal sulcus meets the precentral sulcus. [strong: classic anatomical landmark] (SS) — [record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/3757945); [SciELO review](https://www.scielo.br/j/anp/a/WmxrbT5XjMjxhyXsSLQ863L/)
- Clinical use is lesion localisation, e.g. small infarcts in the knob causing isolated hand weakness that mimics peripheral nerve injury, and glioma or surgical planning. I found no standard radiology-report template that grades hand-knob "development." [moderate] (SS) — [emDocs case review](https://www.emdocs.net/em3am-cortical-hand-knob-stroke-mimicking-peripheral-nerve-injury/)

### Inferences
- [speculative, but most plausible] "손 발달이 느리다" at about age 9 is most likely a lay memory of "뼈나이(골연령)가 실제 나이보다 어리다," read from a **hand X-ray**. That would be a statement about *skeletal and pubertal timing*, not about the brain's hand area. Brain MRI reports at that age normally comment on structural abnormalities and age-appropriate myelination. I found no evidence that radiologists routinely comment on hand-knob "development."
- [speculative] If bone age *was* delayed, the most common benign explanation in an otherwise healthy child is constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP): later puberty and later growth and strength spurt, with normal eventual adult height. That alone could explain *relatively* poor sports performance in late childhood and early adolescence vs earlier-maturing peers, independently of ADHD. The report should present this as an alternative or additive explanation.
- [speculative] Current evidence does not link ADHD itself or methylphenidate to delayed bone age; ADDUCE even found slight *acceleration*. So a delayed bone age at 9, if real, is not a recognised ADHD signature.
- [speculative] Other possibilities worth listing for the person to check in records: a neuropsychological or occupational-therapy fine-motor test (e.g. pegboard, Beery VMI) reported as "slow hand (fine-motor) development." This would be *functional* and fits ADHD/DCD co-occurrence better than either imaging test.

### Gaps
- No Korean-language clinical guideline text on CDGP or Greulich–Pyle reporting conventions was retrieved. A 대한소아내분비학회 source would be ideal.
- No study was found on bone age in *unmedicated* children with ADHD vs controls beyond the dental/vertebral study.
- No source was found that documents typical Korean pediatric brain-MRI report wording; the claim that reports do not mention hand-knob development rests on absence of evidence.
- Unverified prior knowledge, check before use: ADHD and developmental coordination disorder co-occur in roughly 30–50% of cases (e.g. Kadesjö & Gillberg). Probably covered by another researcher.
