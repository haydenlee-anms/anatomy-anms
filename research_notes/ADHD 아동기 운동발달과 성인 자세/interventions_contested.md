## Q1. Motor interventions in DCD and ADHD+DCD: task-oriented vs process-oriented approaches

### Takeaway
Across the DCD literature, approaches that practise the actual task (task-oriented / activity-oriented: Neuromotor Task Training, CO-OP, general skill training) beat "process-oriented" approaches that try to fix supposed underlying deficits first (sensory integration, kinaesthetic training, perceptual-motor training). The process-oriented pooled effect was close to zero. The caveats: almost all of this evidence is in children aged 5 to 12, study quality is mixed, and a 2017 Cochrane review rated the task-oriented evidence as very low quality.

### Cited Findings
- **Smits-Engelsman et al. 2013 meta-analysis** (Smits-Engelsman BCM, Blank R, van der Kaay AC, et al. "Efficacy of interventions to improve motor performance in children with developmental coordination disorder: a combined systematic review and meta-analysis." *Dev Med Child Neurol* 2013;55(3):229–237). Overall weighted effect size d(w) = 0.56. Task-oriented intervention d(w) = 0.89. Physical and occupational therapies d(w) = 0.83. **Process-oriented intervention d(w) = 0.12.** Task-oriented was significantly higher than process-oriented (p = 0.01) and than comparison conditions (p = 0.006). Conclusion: "process-oriented approaches are not recommended for improving motor performance in DCD". **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [PubMed 23106530](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23106530); [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/34821)
- **Smits-Engelsman et al. 2018 update** (Smits-Engelsman B, Vinçon S, Blank R, et al. "Evaluating the evidence for motor-based interventions in developmental coordination disorder: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Res Dev Disabil* 2018;74:72–102; covered studies from 2012 to 2017). Overall d = 1.06, with a wide range. Benefits were seen for activity-oriented approaches, body-function-oriented approaches *combined with* activities, active video games and small-group programmes. The authors said the results "should be interpreted with caution" because study quality varied and some confidence intervals were large. **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [ACU Research Bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8731x/evaluating-the-evidence-for-motor-based-interventions-in-developmental-coordination-disorder-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)
  - **Counter-evidence / method critique:** the Finnish Current Care (Käypä hoito) evidence summary says no true pooled meta-analysis was performed and no p-values were reported for the effect sizes, so the d = 1.06 figure is imprecise — [Käypä hoito nak09911](https://www.kaypahoito.fi/nak09911)
- **Dose data (2018 review, 30 studies):** median frequency 1.5 sessions/week (range 1–5), over a median 9 weeks (4–18), for a median 12 sessions (5–48). The review found a significant moderate correlation between effect size on standardized tests and intervention duration. **[weak–moderate]** (search summary only) — [ACU Research Bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8731x/evaluating-the-evidence-for-motor-based-interventions-in-developmental-coordination-disorder-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)
- **Cochrane review, Miyahara et al. 2017** ("Task-oriented interventions for children with developmental co-ordination disorder." *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2017;7:CD010914). 15 studies (8 RCTs, 7 quasi-RCTs), 649 children aged 5–12. A pooled analysis of 6 trials (169 children) favoured intervention over no intervention (MD −3.63, 95% CI −5.88 to −1.39) but was rated **very low quality**. A pooled analysis of 2 RCTs alone showed no effect (MD −2.34, 95% CI −7.50 to 2.83; low quality). Risk of bias was moderate to high. The authors said their conclusions "are inconsistent with the beneficial effects reported by previous reviews". No adverse events were reported. **[moderate as a statement of uncertainty]** (search summary only) — [Cochrane summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD010914_task-oriented-intervention-children-developmental-co-ordination-disorder); [Cochrane Library](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010914.pub2/information)
- **Preston et al. 2017** (*Clin Rehabil* 2017;31(7):857–870; included only RCTs with PEDro score ≥7). Of 846 articles, 9 high-quality RCTs (15 interventions) qualified. The most effective reported interventions were "Neuromotor Task Training", "Task-oriented Motor Training" and "Motor Imagery + Task Practice Training". Not all included studies were adequately powered. **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [White Rose eprints 103489](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/103489/)
- **Yu, Burnett & Sit 2018** ("Motor skill interventions in children with developmental coordination disorder: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Arch Phys Med Rehabil* 2018). Searched 1995 to August 2017. 66 studies, 18 pooled. Motor skill interventions improved motor competence in the short term. The exact effect sizes were not retrieved, and the repository record's attribution to this paper is probable but not fully verified. **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [ECU repository](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/4804); [Temple citation](https://sites.temple.edu/rtwiseowlsnew/?p=1981)
- **2025 meta-analysis of RCTs** ("Motor-Based Interventions in Children with Developmental Coordination Disorder: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials", *Sports Medicine – Open*). Task-oriented approaches significantly improved overall motor skills, balance, cognitive function and activity performance. **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [PMC12106291](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12106291/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-025-00833-w)
- **International clinical practice recommendations, Blank et al. 2019** (Blank R, Barnett AL, Cairney J, et al. "International clinical practice recommendations on the definition, diagnosis, assessment, intervention, and psychosocial aspects of developmental coordination disorder." *Dev Med Child Neurol* 2019;61:242–285, doi 10.1111/dmcn.14132; initiated by the European Academy of Childhood Disability; evidence graded with Oxford CEBM levels 1–4). 35 recommendations, 5 of them literature-based on intervention. New for this edition: active video games as **adjuncts** to activity- and participation-oriented interventions. **First international recommendations to cover adolescents and adults**, with two new recommendations for them (one good-clinical-practice point, one evidence-based). It concludes there is sufficient evidence that DCD is lifelong. **[strong for "DCD is lifelong"; strong for the task/activity-oriented preference in children]** (search summary only; exact recommendation wording not retrieved) — [Brunel BURA](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/22183); [Univ. South Wales](https://pure.southwales.ac.uk/en/publications/international-clinical-practice-recommendations-on-the-definition/); [CORE](https://core.ac.uk/works/7988422)
- **CO-OP format and scope (UK Royal College of Occupational Therapists):** RCOT describes strong evidence that CO-OP helps young people with DCD reach their goals and transfer what they learn. It notes that outcomes may differ when DCD co-occurs with another diagnosis, such as ADHD. Typical format: 10–12 weekly sessions of 45–60 minutes, with homework. CO-OP "has been extended for use with several other populations, both children and adults." **[moderate for children; weak for adults]** (search summary only) — [RCOT document](https://www.rcot.co.uk/media/660/download)
- A 2022 randomized waitlist-controlled CO-OP trial in children aged 8–12 (1 hour weekly for 10 weeks) found that **both** groups improved significantly, so the advantage over waiting was not clearly shown. **[weak]** (search summary only; the URL is the probable match among the results) — [PMC9082975](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9082975)

### Inferences
- The best-supported principle is **specificity**: practise the target functional movement in context, with problem-solving (CO-OP) or graded task practice (NTT). The "fix the substrate first" logic of process-oriented approaches does not produce motor gains beyond comparison conditions (d ≈ 0.12).
- The pooled effects in children are moderate to large, but the Cochrane review shows how fragile they are once only randomized trials are kept. "Moderate evidence, likely real but smaller than headline effect sizes" is a fair summary.
- ADHD+DCD is a specific subgroup. RCOT flags that comorbidity may change CO-OP outcomes, and there are few intervention trials restricted to ADHD+DCD. Extrapolating DCD findings to ADHD-related motor problems is reasonable but indirect. **[speculative]**

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the exact wording or evidence levels of the Blank 2019 recommendations for adolescents and adults. Full text: doi 10.1111/dmcn.14132.
- I found no meta-analysis restricted to ADHD+DCD intervention trials.
- No adult DCD intervention RCT was found (see Q2 and Q7).

## Q2. Motor learning in DCD/ADHD: explicit vs implicit, practice dose, feedback; can adults still improve?

### Takeaway
DCD involves a measurable deficit in practice- and context-dependent motor learning and in predictive control ("internal modelling"). The available data suggest learning is slower and needs more practice rather than being impossible. Explicit, instruction-heavy training may add cognitive load that hurts. Adult-specific evidence is thin: small lab studies show adults with DCD learn less than controls in implicit sequence tasks, and **no adult DCD training trial was found.**

### Cited Findings
- **Wilson et al. 2013 meta-analysis** (Wilson PH, Ruddock S, Smits-Engelsman B, Polatajko H, Blank R. "Understanding performance deficits in developmental coordination disorder: a meta-analysis of recent research." *Dev Med Child Neurol* 2013;55(3):217–228; literature from 1997 to 2011). Citing papers attribute to it the finding that children with DCD have deficits in motor prediction and online control, the "internal modelling deficit" hypothesis. **[moderate]** (search summary only; the abstract as retrieved did not itself state the internal-modelling conclusion) — [ACU Research Bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/864y5/understanding-performance-deficits-in-developmental-coordination-disorder-a-meta-analysis-of-recent-research)
- **Subara-Zukic et al. 2022 combined systematic review and meta-analysis** (*Front Psychol*; 100 papers from 2016–2021). The most profound DCD deficits were in voluntary gaze control during movement, cognitive-motor integration, **practice-/context-dependent motor learning**, and internal modelling. **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [PMC8829815](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8829815/)
- **Adults, implicit sequence learning (Sinani et al., accepted article, *Adv Neurodev Disord*):** 11 adults with DCD vs 18 matched controls on a serial reaction time task. Controls got gradually faster across blocks. The DCD group **failed to improve during the learning phase** and acquired significantly less sequence knowledge. **[weak]** (small n; a lab task, not training) (search summary only) — [Univ. Birmingham repository PDF](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/188487696/Sinani_et_al_Implicit_motorsequence_learning_in_adults_with_and_without_DCD_AiND_Accepted_Article.pdf)
- **Adults, motor profile (Cousins & Smyth 2003):** 19 adults with DCD or self-reported motor impairment performed worse than controls on all tasks. Slowness and variability of movement were pervasive. This was a cross-sectional study with no training. **[weak–moderate]** (search summary only) — [Lancaster eprints](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/18770)
- **Explicit vs implicit training in children with DCD (Ghent conference abstract, 2012):** compared implicit and explicit sequence training. The authors concluded that "the additional cognitive load imposed by explicit training is not a good strategy" in DCD motor-learning rehabilitation. **[weak]** (abstract only) — [Frontiers event abstract](https://www.frontiersin.org/10.3389/conf.fnhum.2012.210.00118/1614/Belgian_Brain_Council/all_events/event_abstract)
- **Errorless (low-error) practice** facilitated implicit motor learning in typically developing 11-year-olds and in children with ASD. These are not DCD studies, so the evidence is indirect. **[weak]** (search summary only) — [J Sport Motor Dev Learn (Univ. Tehran)](https://jsmdl.ut.ac.ir/article_69916_en.html); [EBSCO record, ASD errorless training](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/education/8c41cce1-2153-586e-a29a-37ffa5289506/errorless-training-benefits-motor-learning-and-kinematic-outcomes-in-children-with-autism-spectrum-disorders)
- **Dose in children:** in DCD trials, the effect size on standardized tests correlated moderately with intervention duration (median 12 sessions over 9 weeks). **[weak–moderate]** (search summary only) — [Smits-Engelsman 2018, ACU](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8731x/evaluating-the-evidence-for-motor-based-interventions-in-developmental-coordination-disorder-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)
- **A 2016 child training study** showed gains in both practised and non-practised skills, which suggests some transfer. **[weak]** (search summary only; citation details not retrieved) — [PubMed 26703915](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26703915/)

### Inferences
- In DCD, "the deficit limits learning" and "the deficit slows learning" are not fully separable in the current data. The child intervention literature, where gains appear and correlate with duration, supports "slower, needs more practice". The adult implicit-learning lab data (Sinani) suggest that some learning mechanisms really are impaired in adults. **[speculative synthesis]**
- Practical implication for an adult self-rehabilitator (extrapolated, **[speculative]**): use more repetitions and longer programmes than a typical person would need; keep verbal rules and internal cues minimal (less cognitive load); practise the actual target movements rather than abstract drills; accept slower progress curves.
- Whether ADHD *without* DCD impairs motor learning per se was not established in the sources I reached.

### Gaps
- One search summary mentioned a 2026 systematic review concluding that children with DCD "can learn implicitly, but more slowly and with more practice", and that implicit, multisensory practice is more effective. I could not identify its citation or URL, so it is excluded from the findings.
- I found no trial of motor-skill **training** in adults with DCD, and no study of feedback frequency or attentional focus (internal vs external) specific to DCD or ADHD.
- No data were retrieved on motor learning in adults with ADHD alone.

## Q3. Effect of stimulants (methylphenidate) on motor control: handwriting, coordination, balance, posture, "muscle tone"

### Takeaway
Small double-blind crossover trials in children with ADHD+DCD show that methylphenidate (MPH) improves standardized motor scores on average. But only about a quarter to a third of children improve to a clinically significant degree, and performance usually stays below that of controls. Handwriting results are mixed. Balance and postural sway improved in some studies. I found **no placebo-controlled motor-outcome data in adults.** The "muscle tone" claim rests on one assessment tool (MFNU) whose validity its own developer's group says still needs research.

### Cited Findings
- **Flapper, Houwen & Schoemaker 2006** ("Fine motor skills and effects of methylphenidate in children with attention-deficit-hyperactivity disorder and developmental coordination disorder." *Dev Med Child Neurol* 2006;48(3):165). 12 children with ADHD+DCD (mean age 9.8) and 12 matched controls. Outcomes: MABC manual dexterity, the BHK handwriting scale and a computerised graphomotor task. A partial abstract reports MPH improved hand fluency and dexterity. A later review summarises: "Impairment in manual dexterity and poor quality of handwriting and drawing improved after MPH use, but performance remained poorer than in the control group" (that summary is most likely about this study but is not confirmed). **[weak]** (n = 12; search summary only) — [Cambridge Core record](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/developmental-medicine-and-child-neurology/article/fine-motor-skills-and-effects-of-methylphenidate-in-children-with-attentiondeficithyperactivity-disorder-and-developmental-coordination-disorder/FBD6CF347709A9AB39A72F8396D85F7C); [review, PMC5523042](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5523042)
- **Bart et al. 2010** (*Res Dev Disabil*; double-blind, placebo-controlled; 18 children with ADHD+DCD). Motor performance on MPH was significantly better than on placebo on all M-ABC subtasks **except static balance**. **Clinically significant improvement in only 33%** of children. **[weak–moderate]** (search summary only) — [Univ. Haifa CRIS](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/a-preliminary-study-on-the-effect-of-methylphenidate-on-motor-per/)
- **Bart et al. 2013** ("Influence of methylphenidate on motor performance and attention in children with developmental coordination disorder and attention deficit hyperactive disorder." *Res Dev Disabil* 2013;34:1922–1927). 30 children aged 5–12 with ADHD+DCD, tested with and without MPH on the MABC-2 and a continuous performance test. Motor and attention scores were both significantly better on MPH. The authors say more research is needed to tell whether motor gains are direct or mediated by attention. **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [Univ. Haifa CRIS](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/influence-of-methylphenidate-on-motor-performance-and-attention-i/)
- **Soleimani et al. 2017** (*Iran J Med Sci*; randomized double-blind crossover; 17 children with ADHD+DCD). MPH improved motor function in **26.6%** of children. **[weak]** (search summary only) — [IJMS](https://ijms.sums.ac.ir/article_40463.html)
- **Stray et al. 2009** ("Methylphenidate improves motor functions in children diagnosed with Hyperkinetic Disorder." *Behav Brain Funct* 2009;5:21). 25 drug-naïve boys aged 8–12, double-blind crossover. Significant improvement on **all 17 MFNU subtests** 1–2 hours after MPH, with no placebo effect. The strongest response was on subtests measuring neuromotor inhibition or "heightened muscular tone in the gross movement muscles". With a 10 mg dose, the effect appeared 0.5–1 hour after dosing and faded rapidly after 3–4 hours. **[weak–moderate]** (search summary only) — [UiS Brage](https://uis.brage.unit.no/uis-xmlui/handle/11250/185947); [BBF PDF mirror](https://0-behavioralandbrainfunctions-biomedcentral-com.brum.beds.ac.uk/counter/pdf/10.1186/1744-9081-5-21.pdf)
  - **Caveat on the "muscle tone" construct:** the MFNU was developed from clinical observation over 25 years, and the author's own thesis states it "is still in need of further validation research, particularly regarding the use in diagnostic assessment". — [UiS Brage thesis record](https://uis.brage.unit.no/uis-xmlui/handle/11250/185947)
- **Postural stability, Bucci et al. 2016** ("Postural Instability in Children with ADHD Is Improved by Methylphenidate." *Front Neurosci* 2016;10:163). 38 children with ADHD and 38 controls, force-platform testing before and after **one month** of MPH. At baseline the ADHD group was less stable than controls. After MPH, spatial and temporal postural stability improved, especially when visual or proprioceptive input was misleading. The authors propose MPH facilitates cerebellar processing. **There was no placebo arm (pre/post design).** **[weak–moderate]** (search summary only) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00163/full); [PMC4854903](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4854903/)
- **Buderath et al. 2009** (cited by Bucci): children with ADHD and children with cerebellar lesions both had poorer posture than controls, milder in ADHD. The ADHD children were reportedly on MPH at testing and showed minor balance and stepping problems resembling mild cerebellar dysfunction. **[weak]** (secondary description only) — [Bucci 2016, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2016.00163/full)
- **Jacobi-Polishook, Shorer & Melzer 2009** (*J Neurol Sci*): a double-blind randomized controlled trial of MPH on postural stability under single- and dual-task conditions in children with ADHD. **Results not retrieved.** — [BGU PDF](https://fohs.bgu.ac.il/Recanati/Physiotrapy/Pub_for_students/the%20effect%20of%20MPH%20on%20Postural%20Stability%20in%20ADHD.pdf)
- **Naturalistic, open-label (Asan Medical Center, Korea, 2015–2020; *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2023):** MPH and atomoxetine both improved DCDQ fine motor/handwriting, general coordination and total scores over 12 weeks. There was no placebo group, and the DCDQ is a parent questionnaire. **[weak]** (search summary only) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00787-023-02144-5)
- **Handwriting is mixed:** one study found legibility improved 3 months after starting medication, but most children kept legibility problems. Another found MPH-treated legibility did not differ from controls. One found sentence-writing fluency was *worse* on MPH than after withdrawal. **[weak, conflicting]** (search summary only; the individual studies were not identified by name) — [review, PMC5523042](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5523042)
- **Adults:** I found no placebo-controlled trial measuring fine-motor coordination, balance or posture in adults with ADHD. The 1985 adult crossover trial (Wender et al., *Am J Psychiatry* 1985;142:547) measured attention, motor *overactivity*, affective lability and impulsivity, not coordination. A registry entry "The Effects of a Single Dose of Methylphenidate on Motor Performance" (NCT04283604) had no details I could confirm. — [AJP abstract](https://ajp.psychiatryonline.org/doi/abs/10.1176/ajp.142.5.547); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04283604](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04283604)

### Inferences
- The consistent pattern is that MPH partly improves motor performance in children with ADHD(+DCD) but does not normalise it, and only a minority show clinically meaningful change. This fits a model in which some of the "motor problem" in ADHD is attentional or inhibitory (drug-responsive) and some is a separate DCD-type deficit (not drug-responsive). **[moderate for the pattern; speculative for the mechanism]**
- No evidence was found that MPH changes posture in the structural sense (spinal alignment). The posture data concern postural *sway/stability*. Claims that stimulants "normalise muscle tone" rest on MFNU findings from one Norwegian group with an under-validated tool. **[weak]**
- For an adult: any motor benefit of medication should be expected to be dose-timed (Stray: about 1–4 hours after a 10 mg dose) and modest. Practising motor skills while medicated could plausibly help attention to practice, but this is untested. **[speculative]**

### Gaps
- The full Flapper 2006 results (BHK effect sizes, whether MABC normalised) were not confirmed.
- The Jacobi-Polishook 2009 RCT results were not retrieved.
- I found no adult motor-outcome RCT of MPH, and no data on long-term (months to years) motor effects.

## Q4. Resistance training and motor-control training in children with ADHD: motor competence, strength, posture

### Takeaway
I found no RCT that isolates resistance training in children with ADHD. Small trials of mixed exercise (jump rope, ball games, HIIT, balance training, table tennis) report gains in fitness, strength, balance and fundamental motor skills, but samples are tiny (n = 12–30) and mostly unblinded. I found nothing on posture outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- **12-week jump-rope and ball programme:** pilot RCT with 12 boys with ADHD (6 exercise, 6 control), 3 sessions a week. Gains in cardiorespiratory endurance, muscle strength, muscle endurance and flexibility. The abstract reports general muscle strength, **not a grip-strength figure.** The previously circulated "grip 10.6 → 12.6 kg" figure could not be verified from the abstract. **[weak]** (search summary only) — [PMC4616125](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4616125)
- **7-week balance training:** randomized study of 30 children with ADHD, 3 sessions a week vs normal activities. Static and dynamic balance (Biodex) improved in the training group and not in controls. **[weak]** (search summary only) — [Iranian J Pediatrics, Brieflands](https://brieflands.com/articles/ijp-95542.html)
- **Combined exercise training (Demircioğlu Karagöz, Hacettepe, 2023):** RCT with physiotherapist-supervised, parent-supervised and control arms, **10 children per group**, measuring motor proficiency and fitness. Results not retrieved. — [Hacettepe open access](https://openaccess.hacettepe.edu.tr/handle/11655/29292)
- **Registered trial NCT04279652:** supervised vs home exercise, 60 minutes 3×/week for 8 weeks, including 10 minutes of strengthening plus aerobic and balance work. No results posted. — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04279652)
- **HIIT RCT in boys with ADHD (2018):** HIIT added to standard multimodal therapy vs standard therapy alone, with motor skills assessed on the MABC-2. Results not retrieved. — [BISp-SURF record](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201710008682)
- **Network meta-analysis (Ouyang et al. 2026, 26 RCTs, children with ADHD):** mind-body exercise ranked best and showed significant benefits for inhibitory control, executive function **and gross motor skills**. **[moderate–weak]** (search summary only) — [Frontiers Psychol 2026 PDF](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1748777/pdf)

### Inferences
- Exercise of almost any structured kind seems to improve fitness and some motor measures in children with ADHD, but the trials cannot separate specific training effects from general activity or attention effects. **[weak]**
- Nothing found supports the claim that strength training *in childhood* would have prevented adult postural problems. That claim is untested. **[speculative]**

### Gaps
- I found no ADHD-specific resistance-training RCT, no posture outcomes in any ADHD exercise trial, and no adult ADHD strength or motor-control training trial.

## Q5. Exercise as an ADHD treatment (the reverse direction): effect sizes, which types work, mechanisms

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses consistently report small-to-moderate benefits of physical activity for executive functions (inhibition, working memory, flexibility, SMD ≈ 0.45–0.5) and small benefits for core symptoms in children and adolescents with ADHD. The best-designed (low risk-of-bias) trials are few, and robustness is limited. **Cognitively engaging exercise** (ball games, martial arts, mind-body practice) appears to beat plain aerobic exercise for higher-order executive functions. Adult evidence is very sparse. Exercise is not shown to replace standard treatment. Mechanisms (catecholamines, BDNF) are well supported in animals and only plausible in humans.

### Cited Findings
- **2023 meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD42023388409): 24 studies, 914 participants**, children and adolescents with ADHD. Physical activity improved inhibitory control (SMD −0.50, 95% CI −0.71 to −0.29), working memory (SMD −0.50, −0.83 to −0.16) and cognitive flexibility (SMD −0.45, −0.81 to −0.09). The negative sign reflects scoring direction, and the authors interpret the effects as benefits. Intensity, motor-skill type, number of sessions and weekly volume moderated the effects. **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [PMC10434964](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10434964/)
- **Separate working-memory meta-analysis:** SMD = 0.51 (95% CI 0.34–0.69). Moderate sessions of 45–60 minutes, over 8–12 weeks, no more than twice a week, worked best. **[moderate]** (search summary only; reported via the ADHD Evidence Project aggregator) — [ADHD Evidence blog, Sept 2025](https://www.adhdevidence.org/blog/meta-analysis-reports-gains-in-working-memory-from-physical-activity-for-children-and-adolescents-with-adhd)
- **2025 meta-analysis of 11 RCTs, ages 6–12, cognitive flexibility:** medium-to-large overall effect (388 children). **No benefit from aerobic exercise alone** (running, jumping). **Large effect for cognitively engaging exercise** (9 studies, 301 children; e.g., soccer, water sports). Correction for publication bias did not change the result. **[moderate]** (search summary only, via aggregator) — [ADHD Evidence blog, May 2025](https://adhdevidence.org/blog/seven-new-meta-analyses-suggest-wide-range-of-benefits-from-exercise-for-persons-with-adhd)
  - **Counter-evidence on flexibility:** another meta-analysis found a narrowly *non-significant* small effect with positive scoring (8 RCTs, 268 children), and a medium effect only with reverse scoring (11 RCTs, 452 children). — [ADHD Evidence blog, Dec 2025](https://adhdevidence.org/blog/meta-analysis-reports-executive-function-gains-from-exercise-interventions-for-adhd)
  - **Counter-evidence on robustness:** a 2025 network meta-analysis of 21 RCTs found cognitive-flexibility estimates "significantly influenced by individual studies, demonstrating limited robustness". Its authors said clinical implementation should be individualised and should "await high-quality validation". — [PMC12916422](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12916422/)
- **Risk of bias in the core-symptom literature (Chen et al., RCTs through July 2022):** 22 trials, **only 3 rated low risk of bias** and 19 moderate. Small-to-medium reduction in core symptoms (11 RCTs, 514 participants). **[moderate–weak]** (search summary only, via aggregator) — [ADHD Evidence blog, Feb 2024](https://www.adhdevidence.org/blog/meta-analysis-suggests-regular-exercise-improves-core-symptoms-and-executive-functions-in-child-and-adolescent-adhd)
- A meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (searched to July 2024) found physical activity alleviated ADHD symptoms, Hedges' g = −0.37 (95% CI −0.72 to −0.02; I² = 45.2%). The interval barely excludes zero. **[weak–moderate]** (search summary only; the exact paper among the results was not pinned down) — [ADHD Evidence exercise tag page](https://adhdevidence.org/blog-tags/exercise)
- A 2026 review noted that neither this meta-analysis nor others show exercise can **replace** standard treatments for core ADHD symptoms. — [ADHD Evidence blog, Mar 2026](https://adhdevidence.org/blog/meta-analysis-of-exercise-interventions-for-children-and-adolescents-reports-medium-to-large-improvements-in-inhibitory-control-with-caveats)
- **Types:**
  - Mind-body exercise ranked top for inhibitory control, executive function and gross motor skills in a 26-RCT network meta-analysis. **[moderate–weak]** — [Frontiers Psychol 2026 PDF](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1748777/pdf)
  - A 2024 mind-body exercise meta-analysis (Peng et al.) found improved **attention** but **no** evidence of benefit for executive function, emotional problems or hyperactivity/impulsivity. **[moderate–weak]** (this partly contradicts the network meta-analysis above) — [Frontiers Psychiatry 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2024.1490708/full)
  - Martial arts ranked among the most effective for executive functions in a 31-RCT network meta-analysis (Li et al. 2023, *J Affect Disord*). An 18-month taekwondo RCT improved selective and sustained attention and inhibitory control in adolescents. **[weak]** (both known only via a narrative review) — [Martial Arts and ADHD narrative review](https://www.tmfv.com.ua/journal/article/view/3360)
  - Cognitively engaging exercise beat aerobic exercise for sustained attention in a 2025 meta-analysis of 11 studies. **[weak–moderate]** (search summary only) — [ADHD Evidence blog, May 2025](https://adhdevidence.org/blog/seven-new-meta-analyses-suggest-wide-range-of-benefits-from-exercise-for-persons-with-adhd)
- **Adults:**
  - Dinu et al. 2023 (*Behav Sci*), 82 adults with ADHD and 77 controls: a single 10-minute bout of cycling or of Hatha yoga improved temporal impulsivity, with yoga helping only the ADHD group. There was **no effect on attention, cognitive or motor impulsivity, or movement.** **[weak]** (acute effects only) — [PMC9952527](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9952527/)
  - The Swedish START trial (*Front Psychiatry* 2025): 12-week structured exercise vs treatment as usual, randomized 2:1, **41 analysed (30 vs 11)**, single clinic. ADHD symptoms improved on the ASRS-v1.1 (self-report). Preliminary. **[weak]** — [Frontiers Psychiatry 2025](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1690216/text)
- **Mechanisms:** in rodents, exercise consistently changes central neurotransmitters and neurotrophins after single bouts, and in ADHD animal models catecholamine and BDNF rises are linked to better cognition. Human findings are "more heterogeneous", mostly use peripheral (plasma/serum) measures, and brain imaging is sparse and conflicting (one PET study found no dopamine change). **[speculative for humans]** — [Borderline Personal Disord Emot Dysregul 2019 review, PMC6945516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6945516); [BMC full text](https://bpded.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40479-019-0115-2)
  - A 2013 child/adolescent review (16 papers) found the BDNF–ADHD association "remains unclear in human beings". — same source as above (search summary only)

### Inferences
- The reverse direction (exercise → better ADHD) is supported at the level of **small-to-moderate executive-function and symptom improvements in children** [moderate]. It is **barely tested in adults** [weak], and it is **not a substitute for standard treatment** [moderate].
- For someone who is also doing motor rehabilitation, the most relevant finding is that **cognitively engaging, skill-rich exercise** (ball sports, martial arts, mind-body practice) outperforms repetitive aerobic work on higher-order cognition. This conveniently matches the motor-learning principle of task-rich practice from Q1. **[speculative overlap]**
- Effect sizes partly depend on scoring direction and on single studies, and many pooled trials are unblinded with subjective outcomes. Headline SMDs of about 0.5 are likely overestimates. **[inference]**

### Gaps
- The coordinator's prior claim that "low risk-of-bias RCTs show small, non-significant effects" was not directly confirmed. What was confirmed: only 3 of 22 trials were low risk (Chen et al.). A sensitivity analysis restricted to those trials was not retrieved.
- I found no adult meta-analysis and no adult trial with objective executive-function or motor outcomes beyond acute single bouts.

## Q6. Contested claims and their evidence status

### Takeaway
None of the popular "fix the underlying neurology" programmes (primitive-reflex integration/INPP/Rhythmic Movement Training, Ayres sensory integration for motor or ADHD outcomes, the Dore/DDAT cerebellar exercise programme, Brain Balance, Interactive Metronome, vision therapy, chiropractic) has convincing controlled-trial evidence for ADHD or motor outcomes. Some have one or two positive but low-quality or developer-affiliated trials. Professional bodies (AAP 2012; AAP/AAO/AAPOS/AAC 2009) explicitly say the evidence is limited or inconclusive, or that the therapy is not endorsed.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Retained primitive reflexes and reflex-integration therapies (INPP, Rhythmic Movement Training)**
- **Konicarova, Bob & Raboch 2013** (*Neuropsychiatr Dis Treat* 2013;9): 35 medication-naïve girls aged 8–11 with ADHD vs 30 controls. Persisting ATNR/STNR correlated with ADHD symptoms. Correlational, with the authors calling it preliminary. **[weak]** — [Dove Press full text page](https://www.dovepress.com/persisting-primitive-reflexes-in-medication-naiumlve-girls-with-attent-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-NDT)
- The same group published further papers: Activitas Nervosa Superior 2012 (Moro and Galant reflexes), and Bob, Konicarova & Raboch 2021 in *Front Psychiatry* (girls and boys). **[weak, single group]** — [Springer ANS 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2FBF03379591); [Frontiers Psychiatry 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.430685/full)
- **2023 systematic review and meta-analysis** ("Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder is associated with (a)symmetric tonic neck primitive reflexes", *Front Psychiatry* 2023, doi 10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1175974): **only 4 studies, 229 participants.** ATNR summary r = 0.48 (95% CI 0.27–0.64), STNR r = 0.39 (0.25–0.52). The ADHD measure, sex and reflex test moderated the results. The authors say longitudinal or experimental studies are needed for causality. **[weak]** (correlational, tiny base) (search summary only) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1175974/full); [PMC10361412](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10361412/)
- An independent group (Holmes, Handloser & Hanley 2016) studied ATNR/STNR/TLR in psychiatric inpatients with ADHD or ASD, using a cross-sectional design from routine clinical data. So it is not *only* the Czech group, but the design is weak. **[weak]** (search summary only) — [Move Play Thrive summary page](https://www.moveplaythrive.com/learn-more/item/retained-primitive-reflexes-in-adhd-and-asd-among-children-in-an-inpatient-psychiatric-setting) (note: this host promotes reflex therapy)
- **McPhillips, Hepper & Mulhern 2000** ("Effects of replicating primary-reflex movements on specific reading difficulties in children: a randomised, double-blind, controlled trial." *Lancet* 2000;355(9203):537–541). According to a 2003 Trinity College thesis summary, the movement programme significantly reduced persistent ATNR and improved reading. This is the strongest single positive trial for the reflex approach, but it targeted **reading**, not ADHD or motor control. I could not verify the outcome data directly. **[weak–moderate; unverified]** — [PubMed 10683004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10683004/); [TCD thesis](https://edepositireland.ie/handle/2262/79670)
- **INPP Norway 2013:** 87 children aged 5–16, quasi-experimental. Reflex status and motor skills improved, and the authors themselves flag "weak validity". **[weak]** — [Fysioterapeuten](https://www.fysioterapeuten.no/neuro-physiological-training-for-children-with-motor-difficulties/122078)
- **Systematic review of reflex-integration interventions:** studies are "relatively few and of moderate quality". They generally improve reflex inhibition and motor skills, but "statistically significant evidence of appreciable impact on children's academic performance and cognitive abilities is lacking." **[weak]** (search summary only) — [Remedy Publications PDF](https://www.remedypublications.com/open-access/what-is-the-effectiveness-of-treatment-for-retainednbspprimitive-10203.pdf)
- **Rhythmic Movement Training (Blomberg):** no RCT for ADHD found. Supporting material is largely case reports on promoter sites. A small 12-week rhythm/balance/coordination exercise study (15 ASD and 12 ADHD children) reported Conners 3 improvements in the ADHD group. Whether it had a control group was unclear, and it did not use the Blomberg protocol. **[weak]** — [PMC12384944](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12384944/); [Move Play Thrive (promoter)](https://moveplaythrive.com/resources/blog/doctors-1-solution-for-adhd)
- Registered trials still in progress: NCT06030310 (Catalonia, feasibility, 50 children) and NCT05825248 (Aberdeen preschool). — [NCT06030310](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06030310); [NCT05825248](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05825248)

**(b) Ayres Sensory Integration (ASI) and sensory-based therapies**
- **AAP policy statement 2012** (AAP Section on Complementary and Integrative Medicine and Council on Children with Disabilities. "Sensory Integration Therapies for Children With Developmental and Behavioral Disorders." *Pediatrics* 2012;129(6):1186–1189, doi 10.1542/peds.2012-0876). It questions whether a true sensory-pathway "disorder" exists or whether these are features of other conditions. Sensory-based OT "may be acceptable as one of the components of a comprehensive treatment plan". Parents should be told research on effectiveness is "limited and inconclusive". Pediatricians should not use "sensory processing disorder" as a diagnosis and should set functional goals and measure progress during any trial. **[strong as a position statement]** (search summary only) — [AAP PDF mirror (SBP)](https://www.sbp.com.br/fileadmin/user_upload/pdfs/POLICY_STATEMENT-Sensory_Integration-Therapies_Children_With_Developmental-Behavioral_DisordesAAP2012.pdf); [MDedge summary](https://mdedge.com/pediatricnews/article/55084/sensory-integration)
- **Vargas & Camilli 1999 meta-analysis** (*Am J Occup Ther* 53:189–198). SI vs no treatment: ES 0.29, with older studies at 0.60 and recent ones at **0.03**. SI vs alternative treatments: ES 0.09, not different from zero. **[moderate]** (old) — [Kean Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.kean.edu/keanpublications/2799)
- **Leong, Carter & Stephenson 2015 meta-analysis** (30 comparison-group studies): little evidence that SI therapy is effective for any diagnostic group. **[moderate]** (search summary only) — [Macquarie Univ. record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/meta-analysis-of-research-on-sensory-integration-therapy-for-indi/)
- **The "fidelity" defence:** ASI proponents argue that reviews such as Camarata et al. 2020 wrongly pooled ASI with sensory diets and single-sense protocols. Schoen et al. 2019, restricted to fidelity-adherent RCTs, found autistic children improved on **individualised goals** (Goal Attainment Scaling) but saw **no benefit for behaviours of concern**. That evidence is in **autism**, not ADHD or DCD, and comes partly from ASI-developer authors. **[weak–moderate, autism only]** — [Frontiers Integr Neurosci 2022 commentary/response](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnint.2022.874320/full); [PMC9211064](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9211064/)
- US insurer technology assessments judge the SI evidence "insufficient to determine the effects of the technology on health outcomes". — [BCBS South Carolina policy](https://www.southcarolinablues.com/web/public/brands/medicalpolicy/external-policies/sensory-integration-therapy-and-auditory-integration-therapy/)
- In DCD, SI belongs to the "process-oriented" class, with a pooled d(w) = 0.12 (Smits-Engelsman 2013). — [PubMed 23106530](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23106530)

**(c) Vestibular/cerebellar exercise programmes: Dore/DDAT, Brain Balance, Interactive Metronome**
- **Dore/DDAT:** Reynolds & Nicolson's 2007 follow-up reported effects on bead threading, balance, rapid naming, semantic fluency and working memory, but **not reading or spelling**. Rack, Snowling, Hulme & Gibbs (2007, *Dyslexia*) concluded "current evidence provides no support for the claim that DDAT is effective in improving children's literacy skills". They cited no placebo control, few children actually diagnosed with dyslexia, misuse of statistics and commercial involvement. Snowling & Hulme (2003) reached the same conclusion about the original trial. In 2008 all 13 UK Dore centres went into administration. The ADHD claims came from company promotion, not trials. **[weak / effectively unsupported]** — [Rack et al. 2007](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/dys.335); [Snowling & Hulme 2003](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/dys.245); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/can-physical-exercise-really-improve-reading-ability); [BPS Psychologist July 2008](https://bps.org.uk/psychologist/news-and-media-july-2008)
- **Brain Balance** (hemispheric integration; balance beams, metronome, swivel chairs, diet): critics say the company gives no evidence that "hemispheric imbalance" characterises ADHD. Its main study (122 children with ADHD, *Int J Adolesc Med Health*) was not rigorous. NPR (2018) reported experts found insufficient proof, that the company overstated its science, and that it often costs more than $10,000 over six months. **[weak]** — [Science-Based Medicine](https://sciencebasedmedicine.org/brain-balance/); [NPR 2018](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2018/06/18/616805015/cutting-edge-program-for-children-with-autism-and-adhd-rests-on-razor-thin-evide)
- **Interactive Metronome, Shaffer et al. 2001** (*Am J Occup Ther* 2001;55(2):155–162): 56 boys with ADHD randomized to IM, video games or no intervention, with reported gains in attention, motor control and some academics. **PEDro score 2/10** (no concealed allocation, no blinding, no ITT, inadequate follow-up). An author is linked to the IM company. **[weak]** — [PubMed 11761130](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11761130/); [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/26700); [USPTO exhibit (conflict of interest)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7122004)

**(d) Vision therapy**
- **Joint statement, AAP/AAO/AAPOS/AAC 2009** ("Learning Disabilities, Dyslexia, and Vision", *Pediatrics* 2009, reaffirmed 2014), with a 2011 technical report (*Pediatrics* 2011;127(3):e818–e856). Vision therapy, tinted lenses and filters are not supported as effective treatments for learning disabilities. "Diagnostic and treatment approaches that lack scientific evidence of efficacy are not endorsed or recommended". There is inadequate evidence that subtle visual problems cause or worsen learning disabilities. The statement addresses learning disabilities and dyslexia. **ADHD is mentioned only in the call for proper educational and psychological assessment.** **[strong for LD/dyslexia; indirect for ADHD]** — [2011 technical report PDF](https://my.imatrix.com/0030255/storage/app/media/e818.full.pdf); [Aetna CPB 0078](https://www.aetna.com/cpb/medical/data/1_99/0078.html); [Reading Rockets](https://www.readingrockets.org/article/learning-disabilities-dyslexia-and-vision)

**(e) Chiropractic**
- **Karpouzis, Bonello & Pollard 2010** ("Chiropractic care for paediatric and adolescent Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder: a systematic review." *Chiropr Osteopat* 2010;18:13). Of 58 citations, only 3 studies concerned paediatric ADHD, all of poor methodological quality, and none met inclusion criteria. Conclusion: "insufficient evidence to evaluate the efficacy of chiropractic care for paediatric and adolescent AD/HD". Notably, this review was written by chiropractic researchers. **[weak / no evidence]** — [Springer/BMC](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1746-1340-18-13); [PMC2891800](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2891800/)

### Inferences
- The common thread: these programmes assume a "root" neurological deficit (retained reflexes, sensory integration, cerebellar or hemispheric imbalance, vision, spinal subluxation) whose correction will cascade into better attention and movement. The DCD intervention literature tested this logic directly (process-oriented vs task-oriented) and found the process-oriented approach close to ineffective (d ≈ 0.12). **[moderate]**
- Retained-reflex claims stand at "association reported, causation and treatment benefit untested". The association itself rests on 4 studies, at least 2–3 of them from one Czech group, using non-standardised reflex tests. **[weak]**
- Some programmes (e.g., INPP, Brain Balance, IM) include balance and coordination exercise. Any motor gains they produce are plausibly explained by **generic practice effects** rather than their stated mechanism. **[speculative]**
- **Safety and harm** are mostly indirect: cost, time, and the opportunity cost of delaying evidence-based care. No direct injury signal was found. **[inference]**

### Gaps
- The previously cited "2020 review saying only the grasp reflex and the extensor plantar response are consistently recognised neurological markers" **could not be identified or verified**. The closest 2020 hit (Volemanová, *Listy klinické logopedie* 2020;4(1):37–44; [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/ee8f1500aebe4d8d992b8df916b76818)) could not be read. Do not cite this claim without checking it.
- I could not identify which 4 studies the 2023 meta-analysis included, so I cannot confirm overlap with the Czech group. The full text is at PMC10361412.
- Brain Balance regulatory action (e.g., an FTC case) was **not checked**, because the search budget was exhausted. Do not assert one.
- I found no controlled trial of vestibular stimulation for ADHD in the results.
- I found no RCT of ASI specifically in ADHD or DCD.

## Q7. Adults with lifelong poor coordination and posture: what rehabilitation evidence supports

### Takeaway
No adult DCD or ADHD motor-training trials were found, so adult guidance must be extrapolated. The strongest adjacent evidence is from chronic low back pain: **all exercise types beat minimal treatment, and "specific" motor-control/stabilisation exercise is no better than general exercise beyond the short term.** So the choice can follow preference, cost and adherence. The link between posture and back pain is weak. Exercise is the mainstay for hypermobility, but its benefit is still uncertain.

### Cited Findings
- **Saragiotto et al. 2016, Cochrane** ("Motor control exercise for chronic non-specific low-back pain." *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2016;1:CD012004): 29 RCTs, 2,431 participants. Motor control exercise (MCE) beat minimal intervention on pain and disability. **Compared with other exercise, there was no clinically important difference** in pain at short-, intermediate- or long-term follow-up, or in short-term disability. Lead author: MCE is "as effective as other types of exercise", so the choice should consider "patient or therapist preferences, cost and availability". **[strong]** (search summary only) — [Cochrane Library](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012004/information); [George Institute](https://georgeinstitute.org/publications/motor-control-exercise-for-chronic-non-specific-low-back-pain); [IWH](https://iwh.on.ca/node/184)
- **Hayden et al. 2021 network meta-analysis** (*J Physiother* 2021;67(4):252–262; 217 RCTs, 20,969 participants). **Every exercise type beat minimal treatment.** Pilates, McKenzie and functional restoration ranked highest for pain and function. A Pilates association says a higher frequency/dose gave larger effects; that source has an interest. **[strong for "exercise works"; moderate for type rankings]** — [UTS OPUS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/173075); [Australian Pilates Association commentary](https://www.pilates.org.au/research-some-types-of-exercise-are-more-effective-than-others-in-people-with-chronic-low-back-pain-a-network-meta-analysis/)
- **Wang et al. 2012 meta-analysis** (*PLoS One*; 5 RCTs, 414 participants): core stability exercise beat general exercise for pain and disability at **short-term** follow-up only, with **no difference at 6 and 12 months.** **[moderate]** — [PMC3524111](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3524111/)
- **Smith, Littlewood & May 2014** (*BMC Musculoskelet Disord* 15:416): a stabilisation-exercise review update, noting stabilisation is the most common UK physiotherapy despite a lack of positive supporting evidence. Pooled results were not retrieved. — [SHU repository](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/11816/)
- **Posture and pain:** a 2010 review by Roffey et al. (summarised by IASP) found "strong and consistent evidence of no association between occupational sitting and low back pain" and no dose-response. A 2022 meta-analysis found that the sitting-vs-standing difference in intradiscal pressure disappears in post-1990 studies. Sit-stand desks gave a small reduction in low-back discomfort (SMD −0.23). **[moderate]** — [IASP](https://www.iasp-pain.org/?p=9087); [PMC8950176](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8950176)
- **Hypermobility (hEDS/HSD):** Palmer et al. 2021 (*Clin Rheumatol*), a systematic review of conservative interventions, included 11 studies (8 controlled trials and 3 cohorts), all exercise-based. It describes exercise as the management mainstay but its benefit as **uncertain**. Reychler et al. found 6 small RCTs (n = 20–57) with highly heterogeneous interventions. **[weak–moderate]** — [PMC7895781](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7895781/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10067-020-05284-0); [UCLouvain (Reychler)](https://dial.uclouvain.be/pr/boreal/object/boreal%3A261170/datastream/PDF_01/view)
- **Adult DCD:** Blank 2019 states DCD is lifelong and adds the first adolescent/adult recommendations (content not retrieved). CO-OP has been extended to adult populations (RCOT). I found no adult DCD training RCT. **[weak]** — [Brunel BURA](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/22183); [RCOT](https://www.rcot.co.uk/media/660/download)
- Adults with DCD perform worse than controls across motor tasks and show impaired implicit sequence learning in small studies (Q2). **[weak]** — [Lancaster eprints](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/18770); [Birmingham PDF](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/188487696/Sinani_et_al_Implicit_motorsequence_learning_in_adults_with_and_without_DCD_AiND_Accepted_Article.pdf)

### Inferences
- For this adult, the best-supported plan is **any structured, progressive exercise they will keep doing**. Specific "core stability" or "motor control" protocols are not shown to beat general exercise for posture-related pain beyond the short term. **[strong for back pain; extrapolated to "posture"]**
- Combine that with motor-learning principles drawn from DCD research: task-specific practice of the movements they actually want (lifting, running, a sport skill), more repetitions and a longer horizon than typical, and minimal reliance on complex verbal rules. **[speculative extrapolation from child data]**
- Cognitively engaging, skill-rich formats (martial arts, ball sports, Pilates or yoga-type practice) plausibly serve both motor goals and ADHD executive-function goals (Q5). This combination is not directly tested in adults. **[speculative]**
- "Fixing posture" as a target in itself has weak support. Posture is weakly linked to pain, so goals are better framed as function, capacity, strength and skill. **[moderate]**
- If hypermobility is present (a common comorbidity in ADHD and DCD; not researched here), exercise is reasonable but its benefits are uncertain, and gradual loading is the usual clinical advice. **[weak]**

### Gaps
- I found no RCT of motor-skill or posture training in adults with DCD or ADHD.
- I found no study linking childhood ADHD/DCD motor problems to *adult* postural alignment.
- Smith 2014 pooled results were not retrieved.
- I found no adult data on whether stimulant medication improves motor learning during practice.
- The link between ADHD and joint hypermobility was not researched in this pass.
